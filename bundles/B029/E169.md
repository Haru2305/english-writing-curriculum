E169｜基準率の落とし穴：目立つ事例だけで確率を判断しない

初見概念「base rate」を本文内で理解し、目立つ個別情報だけで確率を判断しない。

Reading
[1] A factory uses an automatic system to flag machines that may soon fail. The system performs well in testing: among machines that actually fail within a week, it correctly flags most of them. Managers are therefore impressed when a machine receives an alert.
[2] However, failure is rare. In an ordinary week, only a small fraction of machines develop a serious fault. This background frequency is the base rate. Even a reasonably accurate alert system can produce many false alarms when the event it is trying to detect is uncommon.
[3] Suppose a manager hears that the system correctly identifies 90% of machines that will fail. That figure sounds strong, but it does not answer the question the manager actually cares about: given that this machine was flagged, how likely is it to fail? To answer that question, the manager also needs to know how often failures occur in the first place and how often the system flags healthy machines.
[4] The table below illustrates the problem with a fictional group of 1,000 machines.
Table
Actual condition | Machines | Flagged by system
Will fail | 20 | 18
Will not fail | 980 | 98
[5] The system detects 18 of 20 future failures, which is indeed 90%. Yet there are 116 flagged machines in total, and only 18 of them will fail. A manager who focuses only on the 90% detection rate may therefore greatly overestimate what an individual alert means.
[6] This does not make the system useless. An alert can still be valuable if it helps prioritize inspection. But the meaning of the alert depends on both test performance and the base rate of failure. If failure becomes more common in an older group of machines, the same alert system may produce a different proportion of true warnings.
[7] The broader lesson is that evidence about one case must be interpreted against the frequency of the event in the relevant population. A vivid signal can be informative without being decisive.

Q1｜解答例
The base rate is the background frequency of an event in the relevant population before considering the new signal or test result.
Q2｜解答例
Because failure is rare. Even though most future failures are flagged, a small false-positive rate applied to the much larger group of healthy machines produces many flagged machines that will not fail.
Q3｜解答例
A positive alert raises concern and may justify inspection, but it does not by itself show that failure is likely.
Q4｜解答例
The base rate (how often failures occur) and the false-positive rate (how often healthy machines are flagged). The detector works on two groups of very different sizes: it flags most of the small failure group, but false alarms from the much larger healthy group can still outnumber true alerts.
Claim Check
1. Too strong → The 90% figure describes detection among machines that will fail, not failure probability among flagged machines.
2. Supported.
3. Too strong → The alert can still help prioritize inspection even though many flagged machines will not fail.
Model output
Base rates matter because a signal must be interpreted against how common the event already is. In the example, the system detects 90% of future failures, but failures are rare, so false alarms from the much larger group of healthy machines outnumber true alerts. A positive result is therefore useful for prioritizing inspection, but it should not be treated as proof that a machine will fail.

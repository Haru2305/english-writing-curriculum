Passage
A fictional public Wi-Fi network uses a “capacity headroom” target. Headroom is the unused part of network capacity kept available above ordinary demand so that a sudden increase in users does not immediately cause severe slowdown.
At first glance, unused capacity can look inefficient. If average demand is 60% of the network’s maximum capacity, a manager may ask why the remaining 40% is not already being used. The answer is that average demand does not describe short peaks. [A] A network operating close to its limit during ordinary periods has little room to absorb a sudden crowd, a temporary equipment failure, or unusually heavy data use.
Dense Sentence
What gives headroom practical value is not the existence of permanently idle capacity but the fact that a temporary gap between routine demand and maximum sustainable load allows the system to absorb variation whose timing and size cannot be predicted precisely without immediately shifting that variation into slower connections or failed access attempts.
The useful amount of headroom depends on conditions. A network with highly predictable demand and strong backup links may need less. A network serving an event venue or emergency information point may need more because usage can rise rapidly and the cost of failure is higher.
Too much headroom also has a cost. Additional access points, bandwidth contracts, and backup equipment require money even when the spare capacity is rarely used. The goal is therefore not maximum unused capacity but enough reserve to keep performance acceptable during plausible peaks.
Candidate Sentence
This difference between average use and short-term demand creates the need for operational headroom.
FAQ
Q: Does 30% headroom mean the network must always remain exactly 30% unused?
A: No. It is a planning target, not a fixed real-time value.
Q: Can headroom prevent every slowdown?
A: No. Very large surges or equipment failures can still exceed available capacity.
Q: Is average utilization enough to set the target?
A: No. Peak demand, backup reliability, and the consequences of failure also matter.

Q1｜解答例
Capacity headroom is unused network capacity intentionally kept above ordinary demand so that short peaks or temporary disruptions can be absorbed without immediate severe performance loss.
Q2｜解答
A. The previous sentences contrast average utilization with short peaks. The Candidate Sentence names the significance of that difference before the next sentence explains what happens when ordinary operation is already near the limit.
Q3｜解答例
Headroom is useful because routine demand stays below maximum sustainable load. The temporary gap can absorb unpredictable variation. Without that gap, sudden variation is more likely to produce slow connections or failed access.
Q4｜解答例
Zone B, because average utilization is only 61% but the 95th-percentile peak reaches 91%, it lacks a backup link, and event failures are high.
Q5｜解答例
Zone A appears to have relatively strong spare capacity and reliability under observed conditions, but the table does not show whether it would withstand rarer or larger surges.
Model summary
Capacity headroom is spare network capacity kept above ordinary demand to absorb short peaks and temporary disruptions. It improves reliability but costs money because additional capacity may sit unused much of the time. Zone B illustrates why average utilization can be misleading: its average is moderate, yet peak use is high and event failures are frequent. The appropriate reserve therefore depends on peak demand, backup reliability, and the cost of service failure.
Model writing
The operator should set headroom targets using peak demand rather than average utilization alone. It should compare each zone’s 95th-percentile load with maximum capacity and also consider whether a reliable backup link exists. Zone B deserves priority because its peaks approach capacity and event failures are already high. However, the table does not show the duration of peaks, user numbers, or the cost of adding capacity. The operator should therefore test proposed changes against both reliability during busy periods and the financial cost of maintaining additional reserve. A short pilot could test both performance and cost.

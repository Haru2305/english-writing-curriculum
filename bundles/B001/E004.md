Reading
When students achieve different results at school, it is tempting to explain the gap by differences in effort or ability. Individual choices certainly matter, but this explanation can become misleading if it ignores the conditions under which those choices are made.


Consider two students who are equally motivated. One has a quiet room, reliable internet access, books, and adults who can help with difficult assignments. The other shares a small space with several family members, has an unstable connection, and works part-time in the evening. If the second student studies fewer hours, it would be too simple to conclude that the difference is caused only by weaker motivation.


Educational disadvantage can accumulate through several small mechanisms. Limited access to books may make homework slower. Long travel times can reduce the time available for study. Financial pressure may contribute to fatigue or part-time work. None of these factors determines a student's future, but together they can shape the opportunities available. Their effects may also interact. A student who returns home late because of a long commute may have less time to use a shared device before another family member needs it. Missing one assignment may then make the next lesson harder to follow. Small disadvantages can therefore reinforce one another even when no single factor is strong enough to explain the final outcome by itself. This is one reason simple comparisons of effort can be misleading.


This distinction matters for policy. If unequal outcomes are treated only as a problem of individual effort, schools may suggest that students simply study more. Such advice may help some learners, but it does not remove structural barriers. A broader response could include access to study spaces, device lending, tutoring, or flexible support for students with work or family responsibilities.


Recognizing structural factors does not mean denying personal responsibility. Students still make choices, and effort remains important. The point is that the same amount of effort may produce different results under different conditions. A fair explanation therefore asks both what individuals do and what opportunities they actually have. It also avoids treating every group average as a rule about individual students, because people facing similar barriers may respond in very different ways.

### この問題で考えること
結果の差を見て、すぐに「努力の差」と原因推定しないことが核心です。**同じ努力でも、利用できる時間・空間・道具・支援が違えば、結果までの過程が変わる**と考えます。

### 考え方
本文の重要なchainは、
structural barrier  
→ usable study time / resourcesが減る  
→ assignmentやlessonへの参加が難しくなる  
→ disadvantageが積み重なる
です。

さらに、barrierは独立とは限りません。
long commute
→ home arrivalが遅くなる
→ shared deviceを使える時間が減る
→ assignmentを逃す
→ next lessonがさらに難しくなる

このように、**複数の小さな不利が相互作用して結果へつながる**ため、単一原因のcauseより contribute to / may lead to が適切になります。

### 弱い考え方との比較
× Poor students get lower results because they have less money.  
○ Financial pressure can reduce available study time or increase part-time work, which may contribute to lower performance together with other barriers.

改善点は、「属性 → 結果」と飛ばさず、途中のmechanismを示していることです。

### 答案の組み立て
- **Answer**: effort alone cannot fully explain inequality
- **Reason**: students face unequal conditions
- **Mechanism**: those conditions change time, resources, and opportunities to study
- **Support**: commute / part-time work / shared devices
- **Qualification**: effort still matters
- **Return**: explanation should include both choices and structural barriers

### 模範解答の読み方
1文目は「several factors」として単一原因を拒みます。  
2文目はfinancial pressureがstudy timeにどうつながるかを具体化します。  
3文目のsuggestは、原因分析をpolicy responseへ接続しています。つまり、原因を述べるだけでなく、**どのbarrierを減らす支援か**まで戻しています。

### 次の問題に持っていくもの
格差や結果差を説明するときは、  
**individual action + structural condition + interaction between barriers**  
を分けて考えます。  
結果の差だけから原因を一つに決めないことが重要です。

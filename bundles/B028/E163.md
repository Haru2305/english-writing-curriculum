Reading
[1] A sports team tests a new recovery routine after noticing that several players had unusually poor sprint times in one training session. In the next session, most of those players improve. It is tempting to conclude that the routine caused the improvement.
[2] That conclusion may be correct, but another process must also be considered. Performance naturally varies from session to session because of sleep, motivation, minor illness, measurement noise, and many other factors. A player selected for having an unusually poor result is partly selected because several unfavorable influences happened to occur at once.
[3] On a later test, those influences may not all recur. Even without an effective intervention, an extreme result is therefore likely to be followed by a result closer to the player’s usual level. This tendency is called regression to the mean.
[4] The idea does not imply that every improvement after a bad result is meaningless. Rather, it means that before-and-after comparisons can exaggerate an intervention’s apparent effect when participants are chosen because their initial values were unusually high or low.
[5] A similar problem can appear in schools. Suppose extra tutoring is offered only to students who perform especially poorly on one short quiz. Some students may improve on the next quiz partly because the first score reflected temporary difficulty. If improvement is attributed entirely to tutoring, the program’s effect may be overstated.
[6] One way to reduce this uncertainty is to compare with a similar group that was selected by the same rule but did not receive the intervention. Repeated measurements before the intervention can also help show whether the “extreme” starting result was typical or unusual for each person.
[7] The broader lesson is that change after selection is not the same as change caused by treatment. When people are chosen because of extreme measurements, some movement toward ordinary values may occur even if nothing effective is done.

解答・解説・自己修正
Q1｜解答例
Regression to the mean is the tendency for an unusually high or low measurement to be followed by a value closer to a person’s usual level because temporary influences are unlikely to recur in exactly the same way.
Q2｜解答例
The later score may reflect natural variation toward a typical level; the change alone does not identify its cause.
Q3｜解答例
Because people selected for extreme starting values may improve partly through ordinary variation, so part of the observed change can occur without an effective intervention.
Q4｜解答例
Extreme initial performance leads to selection; temporary influences become less extreme later; scores improve; the improvement is then mistakenly attributed entirely to the intervention.
Claim Check
1. Too strong → Extreme scores may reflect real performance plus temporary influences; they are not necessarily errors.
2. Supported.
3. Too strong → Tutoring may have helped, but improvement alone does not show how much of the change it caused.
Model output
Regression to the mean occurs when an unusually extreme measurement is followed by a value closer to the usual level, partly because temporary influences do not repeat exactly. This can make an intervention appear more effective when people were selected for extreme starting scores. A comparison group chosen by the same rule, or repeated measurements before treatment, can help distinguish ordinary variation from a true intervention effect.

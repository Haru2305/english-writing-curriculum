Reference Guide
For it / they / this / such:


1. Check number and grammar.
2. Check what meaning the sentence requires.
3. Look backward for the smallest sufficient referent.
4. For this / such, the referent may be an entire event or idea, not one noun.

Reading
Historians rarely observe the past directly. Instead, they reconstruct it from materials that happened to survive: official documents, letters, tools, buildings, financial records, oral traditions, and many other traces. Because different sources were produced for different purposes, the surviving record does not represent every group equally.


For example, a city archive may preserve tax records created by officials. They can reveal where households lived, what property was registered, or which merchants paid particular fees. Yet such records were not written to describe daily life in a complete or neutral way. They reflect the categories that administrators needed for taxation and control.


Private letters can provide a different perspective. A letter may describe emotions, relationships, or events that official records ignore. This can make it valuable, but the historian still needs to ask who wrote it, for whom, and why. A personal account may be detailed while representing only one person's experience.


Problems become more difficult when one source category survives much better than another. Suppose a port city preserves thousands of merchant records but very few documents from temporary workers. This imbalance can make commercial activity appear better documented than labor conditions. It does not prove that trade was more important to every resident; it shows that the evidence is uneven. Preservation itself can create bias. Documents stored by governments, religious institutions, or wealthy families may survive because those groups had buildings, staff, and reasons to keep records. Objects used by poorer households may disappear because they were repaired, reused, discarded, or never entered an archive. Historians therefore need to distinguish between what was common in the past and what happens to be common in surviving evidence.


Historians therefore compare sources rather than treating one archive as a complete picture. When several independent materials point in the same direction, confidence may increase. When they conflict, the disagreement itself can reveal differences in perspective, purpose, or social position. Comparison also requires attention to time. Two records that describe the same institution may reflect different periods, so an apparent contradiction may represent real change rather than unreliable evidence. The task is not simply to choose the source that seems most detailed, but to ask carefully what each source can and cannot support in its own specific historical and social context.


Such comparison also changes the kinds of questions historians ask. Instead of asking only “What happened?”, they may ask “Who recorded this?”, “Whose experience is missing?”, and “What assumptions shaped the categories in the source?” These questions do not make historical knowledge impossible. They make the reasoning behind an interpretation more visible.

照応設問の考え方
A good answer should identify the content unit, not only repeat the pronoun's nearest noun.


Example
This can make it valuable.
“This” = the letter's ability to reveal experiences that official records may omit.

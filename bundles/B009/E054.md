Text A｜Online rumor correction
A local government posts a correction after a false message claims that drinking water is unsafe. The original rumor used a genuine photograph of repair work but attached it to the wrong neighborhood and date. This made the post look credible even though the conclusion was false. The correction explains where the photograph was actually taken, reports current test results, and links to the laboratory data. It also avoids repeating the rumor as a headline, because simply restating a false claim can increase familiarity. Residents are asked to check future claims against dated notices rather than judging reliability from images alone. The correction page keeps an archive of earlier notices so that residents can compare dates and see whether a screenshot has been removed from its original context. The government also explains how often water is tested and what result would trigger a new warning. This gives readers a standard for interpreting later claims instead of asking them to trust one correction permanently.


Text B｜Historical census records
A historian studies household registers from a rapidly growing industrial town. They contain detailed information about registered residents, occupations, and addresses, but temporary workers were often recorded inconsistently. This gap matters because the factory districts relied heavily on seasonal labor. If the historian treated the registers as a complete list of everyone who lived or worked in the town, the resulting population estimate could be too low. Comparing the registers with factory payrolls and housing records provides a broader picture, although none of these sources is complete by itself. Payrolls may omit unpaid family workers, while housing records can miss people who moved frequently. The historian therefore compares patterns across sources rather than treating any one list as a final count. Differences among sources are recorded explicitly because the disagreement itself may reveal who was easier or harder for officials to record.


Text C｜Preventive-health reminder
A clinic wants to increase attendance at a free screening program. Staff first assume that patients simply forget their appointments, so they propose sending text reminders. A small pilot shows that reminders help some people, but many missed appointments occur because the clinic closes before evening. The team therefore revises its explanation of the problem: forgetting contributes to missed visits, but scheduling is also a major barrier. The clinic keeps the reminder system and adds one late session each week. The revised program addresses two different causes rather than expecting one intervention to solve every problem. After several weeks, the clinic compares attendance by appointment time and by reminder status. If late sessions help more than additional messages, staff can shift limited resources toward practical access barriers rather than simply sending more reminders to everyone in the same way without examining why they missed visits.

Short Writing
モデル
Text B shows that one visible source can produce a poor conclusion when important groups are missing from it. The household registers contain detailed information, but temporary workers were recorded inconsistently. A historian who uses only those records may therefore underestimate the town's population. Comparing several sources makes the limits of each source easier to recognize.


Alternative
Text A shows that a genuine image does not guarantee a reliable conclusion. The photograph was real, but it was attached to the wrong place and date. Readers who judged the claim from the image alone could therefore accept a false message. Reliability requires checking context and supporting evidence.

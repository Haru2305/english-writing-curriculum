Reading
[1] A city clinic introduced an online follow-up survey after routine appointments. Patients received a message asking whether explanations were clear, whether waiting time was acceptable, and whether they would recommend the clinic. After three months, 91% of respondents reported that they were satisfied with the visit.
[2] The number looks impressive, but it does not automatically describe all patients. Only 38% of people who received the survey chose to answer it. The clinic therefore faced a question that applies to many voluntary surveys: are the people who respond similar enough to the people who do not respond?
[3] One possible problem is self-selection bias. This occurs when the decision to participate in a survey is related to the experience being measured. Very satisfied patients may answer because they want to express appreciation. Very dissatisfied patients may also answer because they want to complain. People with moderate experiences may feel less reason to spend time on the survey.
[4] If response behavior differs in this way, the percentage among respondents may not equal the percentage in the whole patient population. Importantly, a low response rate does not prove that the survey is biased. Bias depends on whether responders and non-responders differ in a relevant way. A small but representative sample can be more informative than a larger sample that systematically excludes one type of experience.
[5] The clinic compared basic records and found that older patients were less likely to answer the online survey than younger patients. Because age could be related to communication needs or comfort with digital systems, this difference gave the clinic a reason to be cautious. However, the records did not reveal whether non-responders were more or less satisfied.
[6] The clinic therefore added a small number of phone interviews with randomly selected non-responders. The goal was not to replace the online survey but to check whether its respondents looked unusual. If phone responses showed a very different pattern, the online satisfaction figure would need stronger qualification.
[7] The broader lesson is that survey interpretation requires two questions: what did respondents say, and how did those respondents come to be included? A precise percentage can still give a misleading impression if the process that produced the sample is ignored.

Q1｜解答例
Self-selection bias occurs when people’s decision to participate is related to the experience or characteristic being measured, so respondents may differ systematically from the full population.
Q2｜解答例
It shows that satisfaction was high among respondents, but it cannot by itself establish that 91% of all patients were satisfied.
Q3｜解答例
Because a low response rate creates a risk, not proof, of bias. The key issue is whether responders and non-responders differ in relevant ways.
Q4｜解答例
Survey percentages must be interpreted together with the process that produced the sample.

Model output
Self-selection bias can occur when the decision to answer a survey is related to the experience being measured. In that case, respondents may not represent the entire population even if the percentage among them is precise. The clinic added phone interviews with selected non-responders to check whether the online respondents were unusual. This comparison could show whether the 91% satisfaction figure needs stronger qualification, because a precise percentage can still mislead if the way the sample was formed is ignored.

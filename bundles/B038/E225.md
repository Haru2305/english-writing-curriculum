Part 1｜Reading A

Suppose researchers observe that people who exercise regularly tend to live longer than people who do not. The association is interesting, but it does not by itself show that exercise caused the difference. People who exercise may also differ in diet, income, access to medical care, smoking, sleep, or many other characteristics.

This is the central difficulty of observational research. Researchers observe choices and outcomes as they occur rather than assigning people randomly to different conditions. As a result, the groups being compared may differ before the exposure of interest begins.

Statistical adjustment can reduce some of these differences. If age and smoking are measured, researchers can compare people who are more similar on those variables. But adjustment works only for variables that were measured well enough to be included. An unmeasured factor can still influence both the exposure and the outcome.

Consider a study of coffee consumption and health. People who drink different amounts of coffee may also have different work schedules, stress levels, or sleep patterns. If some of these factors are not measured, an apparent effect of coffee may partly reflect the other differences.

[ A ]

One strategy is to look for situations that create variation resembling random assignment. A policy introduced in one region but not another, a sudden eligibility rule, or a naturally occurring cutoff can sometimes provide groups that differ in the exposure for reasons less closely tied to personal characteristics.

These designs are not automatically unbiased. A regional policy may coincide with other changes, and people just above and below an eligibility threshold may still differ in relevant ways. The usefulness of the design depends on whether its assumptions are plausible.

Researchers also examine whether different methods lead to similar conclusions. A randomized trial, an observational cohort, and a natural experiment each have different strengths and weaknesses. Agreement across designs can be informative because the same hidden bias is less likely to explain every result.

Disagreement is informative too. If an association appears in routine observational data but disappears in randomized evidence, researchers may investigate confounding or differences in the people studied. If a trial finds an effect in a narrow population while observational data show little benefit in everyday practice, adherence or implementation may matter.

Causal reasoning therefore requires more than a large dataset or a sophisticated model. It requires an explicit account of why the comparison can isolate the effect of interest and what alternative explanations remain.

Part 2｜Reading B

Museums and historic sites face a recurring problem: how much should a damaged object or building be restored? Visitors often want to see something coherent enough to understand. At the same time, replacing too much original material can create an object that looks historically complete while containing little of the historical fabric people came to see.

Minimal intervention is one response. Conservators stabilize what survives and replace only what is necessary to prevent further loss. This approach preserves original material and makes later reinterpretation possible, but the object may remain fragmentary or difficult for visitors to imagine in its earlier form.

Reconstruction takes a different approach. Missing parts can be added so that a structure or object becomes easier to understand. If the evidence is strong, reconstruction can make spatial relationships visible in a way that labels or drawings cannot.

The difficulty is uncertainty. Historical evidence rarely specifies every missing detail. Builders may have changed a structure many times, records may conflict, and some materials may be unknown. A reconstruction can therefore make one interpretation look more certain than the evidence justifies.

[ B ]

For this reason, reversibility is often valued. If a new element can later be removed without damaging original material, future conservators can revise the interpretation when better evidence appears. Complete reversibility is not always possible, but the principle encourages caution.

Another strategy is visual distinction. New material can be made subtly different from the original so visitors can recognize what is historical and what is reconstructed. The difference must be noticeable enough to avoid deception but not so dramatic that it prevents people from seeing the object as a whole.

Digital reconstruction offers yet another option. A virtual model can display alternative hypotheses without physically altering the object. However, digital images can also look more certain than the evidence if uncertainty is not shown.

The central issue is therefore not whether restoration is good or bad. It is how to balance preservation, interpretation, and uncertainty while making clear which parts of the visitor’s experience come from surviving evidence and which come from reconstruction.

Model answer (107 words)

I think universities can allow students to use AI tools for written assignments if the rules are specific and the use is disclosed. These tools can help students generate questions, compare possible structures, or identify unclear sentences. However, the final argument should still represent the student’s own reasoning. For example, a student might ask an AI system for counterarguments but then evaluate those ideas and write the response independently. Universities should require students to describe how the tool was used and should prohibit submitting generated text as original work when the assignment is meant to assess writing ability. Disclosure makes responsible assistance easier to distinguish from substitution.

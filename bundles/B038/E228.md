Part 1｜Reading A

Scientific knowledge rarely depends on a single study. Individual studies are conducted with limited samples, particular methods, and specific populations. Even careful research can produce an estimate that is somewhat higher or lower than the effect that would be observed across many repetitions.

For this reason, researchers often combine evidence across studies. A meta-analysis uses a statistical procedure to summarize results from several investigations that address a related question. The combined estimate can be more precise than the estimate from a small study, but only if the collection of studies is informative.

The first difficulty is comparability. Two studies may appear to ask the same question while using different definitions, doses, follow-up periods, or outcome measures. Combining them mechanically can create a number that is precise but conceptually unclear.

A second difficulty concerns which studies become visible. Studies with dramatic or statistically significant results may be more likely to be published, cited, or submitted for publication. Studies finding little difference can remain in file drawers, internal reports, or unfinished manuscripts.

[ A ]

If the visible literature overrepresents large effects, a meta-analysis based only on published studies can inherit that bias. The statistical calculation may be correct while the input evidence is incomplete.

Researchers use several approaches to reduce this problem. Clinical trials can be registered before results are known, making it easier to identify completed studies that were not published. Reviewers can search conference records, trial registries, dissertations, and other sources in addition to journal articles.

Even when every relevant study is found, heterogeneity remains important. A treatment may work differently in younger and older patients, in different health systems, or at different doses. Variation among study results is therefore not always noise to be averaged away. It can reveal conditions under which an effect becomes stronger or weaker.

This is why a useful evidence synthesis reports more than one pooled number. It examines differences among studies, the quality of the methods, the plausibility of publication bias, and the degree to which the included populations match the decision at hand.

The goal of synthesis is not to make uncertainty disappear. It is to organize uncertainty. A combined estimate can clarify the overall pattern while still showing where evidence is weak, inconsistent, or unlikely to generalize.

Part 2｜Reading B

Translation is sometimes imagined as replacing each word in one language with a corresponding word in another. This model works poorly whenever meaning depends on context, convention, or distinctions that one language marks differently from another.

A word can cover several concepts. The English word “home,” for example, can refer to a physical residence, a place of belonging, or an abstract point of return. Another language may divide these meanings among several words or combine them with ideas that English expresses separately.

Grammatical structure also shapes what must be stated. Some languages regularly mark distinctions such as gender, formality, evidential source, or whether an action is completed. Translators may need to make explicit a distinction that the original sentence left implicit, or omit a distinction that cannot be reproduced naturally.

This means that literal similarity and functional equivalence can pull in different directions. A translation that follows the source sentence word by word may preserve surface structure while sounding unnatural or suggesting the wrong relationship between ideas.

[ B ]

Context also includes the purpose of the text. A legal contract, medical instruction, poem, advertisement, and casual message require different priorities. Ambiguity that is acceptable in literature may be dangerous in a dosage instruction. A joke may require replacing the original wording entirely to produce a similar effect.

Back-translation is sometimes used to check important material. A translated text is independently translated back into the original language, and discrepancies are examined. This can reveal missing information, but it does not prove equivalence. Two translators can make compensating choices that produce a similar final wording while intermediate meanings differ.

Consultation with users is therefore valuable. In health surveys or public information, translators may ask members of the target community what a phrase means to them, whether a term is familiar, and whether the tone is appropriate. The goal is not merely linguistic accuracy but successful communication in context.

A strong translation is thus an evidence-based interpretation. The translator must preserve relevant meaning while making justified choices about form, tone, explicitness, and cultural expectations.

Model answer (108 words)
The seminar should focus on evaluating and using evidence across fields. A sample task could ask students to read a short article, identify its main claim, and write a response that supports or challenges the claim with specific evidence. That skill transfers to science, humanities, and professional courses because students in all fields must distinguish claims from support. To avoid becoming extra general workload, the seminar should stay small and use examples chosen by each department. The requirement should assess one transferable process rather than cover many unrelated topics. This would give first-year students a common academic foundation while allowing the content to remain relevant to their field.

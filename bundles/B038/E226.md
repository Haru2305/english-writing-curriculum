Part 1｜Reading A

Queues are often treated as simple evidence of insufficient capacity. If people are waiting, the obvious solution seems to be adding more staff, more service counters, or more appointment slots. Sometimes that is exactly what is needed. But queues are produced by the interaction of several factors, and increasing average capacity does not always remove long waits.

One important factor is variability. Imagine a clinic that can usually serve ten patients per hour and receives, on average, ten patients per hour. If arrivals and service times were perfectly regular, the system might function smoothly. In reality, several patients may arrive together, some consultations take longer than expected, and staff availability changes. Because there is almost no spare capacity, a small temporary imbalance creates a queue that can take a long time to disappear.

This means that a system operating near its maximum average capacity can experience disproportionate delays. A small amount of unused capacity may look inefficient, but it can absorb fluctuations and prevent delays from spreading across the day.

Appointment systems attempt to control arrivals, yet they create their own trade-offs. Longer appointment slots reduce the risk that one complex case delays everyone who follows, but they may leave time unused when cases are simple. Shorter slots increase nominal capacity but can make the schedule fragile.

[ A ]

No-shows add another difficulty. Some services deliberately book slightly more appointments than they can handle because a predictable fraction of people do not attend. If the no-show rate is stable, this may reduce wasted capacity. If nearly everyone arrives on a particular day, however, the same policy creates severe congestion.

Priority rules also affect what a queue means. An emergency department does not simply serve people in the order they arrived. Patients with urgent conditions are treated first, so a person who waits longer is not necessarily being treated unfairly. The system is optimizing a different objective from first-come, first-served.

Digital waiting systems can make queues less visible without reducing the underlying delay. A person may wait at home for a message rather than sit in a room. This can improve experience, but the service still has to distinguish between reducing inconvenience and increasing actual capacity.

Managers therefore need several measures: average waiting time, the longest waits, variability across days, missed appointments, staff utilization, and outcomes for high-priority cases. A single average can hide a small group experiencing extreme delays.

The broader lesson is that queues are dynamic. They reflect not just the number of resources but also uncertainty in arrivals, variation in service time, priority rules, and the amount of spare capacity available to absorb shocks.

Part 2｜Reading B

Citizen science projects invite members of the public to contribute observations to research. Volunteers may record birds, photograph insects, measure rainfall, classify images, or report environmental conditions through a phone application.

The scale can be impressive. Professional researchers cannot be present at every location every day, whereas thousands of volunteers can generate observations across wide areas and long periods. Large datasets can reveal migration timing, rare events, or changes in species distribution.

However, volunteer data are not produced by a fixed sampling scheme. People tend to visit places that are accessible, attractive, or already known to contain interesting species. They may collect more data on weekends, in good weather, or near cities.

Experience also varies. An expert observer may identify a difficult species from a brief sound, while a beginner may record only obvious organisms. New volunteers can improve with practice, so the quality of observations may change over time.

[ B ]

These problems do not make citizen science unusable. Instead, researchers can record information about the observation process itself. How long did a person search? How far did they travel? Did they report every species detected or only unusual ones? What equipment did they use?

Repeated observations can also help. If many observers independently report the same species in a location, confidence increases. Photographs or audio recordings can allow later verification. Automated checks can flag records that are unusual for a particular region or season.

Statistical models can then account for some differences in effort and detectability. But modeling cannot repair information that was never collected. If early versions of a project recorded only the species name and location, later researchers may be unable to distinguish a short casual observation from a systematic two-hour survey.

Project design should therefore anticipate future analysis. Simple interfaces encourage participation, but asking too little can make observations difficult to interpret. Asking too much can discourage volunteers. The best design identifies a small set of metadata that greatly improves scientific value without making participation burdensome.

Citizen science is strongest when the public contribution and the scientific analysis are designed together. The number of observations matters, but so does knowledge of how those observations came to exist.

Model answer (104 words)
Attendance should affect grades only when being present is necessary to demonstrate the skill the course is assessing. In a laboratory, clinical exercise, or discussion class, participation produces evidence that cannot be replaced by reading notes later. A large lecture is different: if students can demonstrate the same knowledge on assignments and exams, attendance itself should not earn points. For example, missing a patient-interview practice session may justify losing participation credit, while missing a recorded lecture should not. Courses should also allow reasonable exceptions for illness or emergencies. The rule should measure required learning activity rather than punish absence as an end in itself.

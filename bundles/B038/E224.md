Part 1｜Reading A

Systems that rarely fail are not necessarily systems in which every component is perfect. In many fields, reliability comes from redundancy: the presence of more than one component capable of performing a necessary function. Aircraft have backup systems, computer servers may store duplicate copies of data, and hospitals may keep alternative sources of power for critical equipment.

At first sight, redundancy appears inefficient. If one component normally performs the task, a second component may remain unused most of the time. Maintaining backup capacity costs money, space, training, and attention. Organizations under pressure to reduce visible waste may therefore be tempted to remove resources that seem unnecessary during ordinary operation.

The difficulty is that ordinary operation is exactly when the value of redundancy is hardest to observe. A backup generator that is never needed can look like an avoidable expense. Its benefit becomes obvious only when the main power supply fails. This creates a familiar asymmetry: the cost of redundancy is visible every day, while the benefit may remain hypothetical for years.

Yet simply adding backups does not automatically create resilience. Two apparently independent systems can share the same vulnerability. A hospital may have two communication networks, but both may depend on the same physical cable. Two data centers may exist in different buildings but rely on one regional power grid. If the shared dependency fails, redundancy disappears at the moment it is most needed.

[ A ]

This is why engineers distinguish duplication from diversity. Duplication means having more than one copy of the same solution. Diversity means having alternatives that fail in different ways. A paper checklist, for example, may serve as a useful backup to an electronic system because it does not require electricity, a network connection, or a functioning display.

Diversity also creates costs. Different systems require different training and may interact in unexpected ways. A rarely used backup may fail because staff have forgotten how to operate it. If emergency procedures are too complex, the existence of multiple alternatives can slow decisions.

Resilience therefore depends on preparation as well as equipment. Backup systems must be tested, responsibilities must be clear, and transitions between normal and emergency operation must be practiced. Organizations sometimes discover during drills that a backup technically works but cannot support the actual workload or that no one has authority to activate it quickly.

There is also a danger of overconfidence. Knowing that a backup exists can encourage people to accept more risk in the primary system. If a backup is imperfect or shares hidden dependencies with the primary system, this confidence can be misplaced.

The strongest design asks several questions: Which failures are plausible? Which components share dependencies? How quickly can a backup take over? What capacity is needed during disruption? And can people actually use the alternative under pressure?

Redundancy is therefore not the opposite of efficiency. It is a way of paying a visible cost in ordinary times to reduce the chance that one failure becomes catastrophic. Whether that cost is justified depends on the consequence of failure and on how genuinely independent and usable the backup is.

Part 2｜Reading B

Sleep is often discussed as if it were a single quantity: the number of hours a person spends in bed. Duration matters, but it does not capture all aspects of sleep.

Two people can both spend eight hours in bed and have very different experiences. One may fall asleep quickly and remain asleep. The other may wake repeatedly, spend long periods awake, or have sleep interrupted by pain, noise, or breathing problems.

Timing matters as well. Human physiology follows daily rhythms that influence alertness, hormone release, body temperature, and many other processes. A person may obtain the same number of hours at different times of day, yet the biological context of those hours can differ.

Regularity is another dimension. A schedule that shifts substantially between weekdays and weekends can repeatedly move sleep to different biological times. For some people, this variation is partly imposed by work or school schedules rather than personal preference.

[ B ]

Researchers therefore use several measures: sleep duration, efficiency, timing, regularity, subjective quality, and sometimes physiological indicators. No single measure captures everything that people mean by “good sleep.”

Measurement method matters too. A questionnaire can describe how rested someone feels but may not estimate sleep stages accurately. A wearable device can collect many nights of data but infers sleep from indirect signals such as movement or heart rate. A laboratory study can provide detailed physiological information but may observe only one or two nights in an unfamiliar environment.

These methods should not be treated as interchangeable. Each answers a somewhat different question. A person can feel that sleep is poor even when one device reports a normal duration, and the disagreement may reflect the limits of the measurement rather than an error by either side.

For practical decisions, the important step is to match the measure to the question. If the concern is an irregular schedule, timing and day-to-day variability may matter more than average duration. If the concern is repeated awakenings, time spent in bed alone provides little information.

Model answer (97 words)

I think some campus facilities should remain open 24 hours, but only when there is clear demand and adequate staffing. Students have different schedules, and a late-night study space can be useful during busy periods or for commuters with long travel times. However, keeping every building open would increase security, energy, and staffing costs. For example, a university could keep one central library area open overnight while closing less-used buildings. Entry could require a student ID, and security staff should be present. This limited system would provide flexibility without treating continuous access as necessary for every facility.

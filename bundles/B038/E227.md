Part 1｜Reading A

Weather forecasts often present a single number: tomorrow’s high temperature, the expected amount of rain, or the predicted path of a storm. Such numbers are useful, but they can hide the fact that forecasting is a problem of uncertainty rather than simple calculation.

Modern forecasting begins with observations of the atmosphere. These observations are extensive but incomplete. Temperature, pressure, wind, and humidity are measured at many locations, yet the atmosphere changes continuously in three dimensions. Measurements also contain error.

Computer models use these observations to estimate the current state of the atmosphere and calculate how it may develop. The equations are based on physical principles, but small differences in the estimated starting state can grow over time. This sensitivity limits how precisely some events can be predicted far in advance.

One response is ensemble forecasting. Instead of running one model from one estimated starting state, forecasters run the model many times with slightly different starting conditions or model assumptions. The collection of results shows a range of plausible futures.

If nearly all ensemble members predict rain in the same region, confidence is relatively high. If the results are widely scattered, uncertainty is greater. The spread itself is information.

[ A ]

A probability forecast can therefore be more informative than a single deterministic statement. A 30 percent chance of rain does not mean that the forecast is “wrong” whenever rain fails to occur. Across many situations assigned a 30 percent probability, rain should occur roughly three times out of ten if the forecasts are well calibrated.

Calibration is different from sharpness. A forecast that always says 50 percent may be well calibrated in some environments but not very useful because it rarely distinguishes high-risk from low-risk situations. A good forecast aims to assign high probabilities when events are truly likely and low probabilities when they are unlikely, while keeping those probabilities statistically honest.

Communication creates another challenge. People often want a yes-or-no answer because decisions are discrete: carry an umbrella or not, cancel an event or continue, evacuate or stay. The forecast cannot remove uncertainty simply because the decision maker must choose one action.

Decision thresholds therefore depend on consequences. A person may carry an umbrella when rain probability is 30 percent because the cost is small. An emergency manager may take action at a lower probability if the potential harm is enormous.

Forecast quality should thus be judged not only by whether one event occurred exactly as predicted but also by how probabilities perform over many cases and whether uncertainty is communicated in a form that supports decisions.

Part 2｜Reading B

Price indexes are designed to summarize changes in the cost of many goods and services with one number. To build an index, statisticians first identify a collection of items that households purchase and assign weights reflecting how important those items are in spending.

If every household bought the same things in the same proportions, interpretation would be relatively simple. In reality, spending patterns differ. A household that rents a home, drives long distances, and has young children faces a different set of prices from a household that owns a home, uses public transport, and spends more on medical care.

A national index therefore describes an average pattern, not the exact experience of every person. When fuel prices rise sharply, households that drive more may experience greater cost pressure than the average suggests.

Weights also change over time. Consumers substitute between goods, new products appear, and services that were once rare become common. If an index continued using an old basket forever, it would gradually represent current spending less well.

[ B ]

Quality change is another difficulty. A computer sold today may cost more than a computer sold years ago but also perform far more functions. Treating the entire price difference as inflation would ignore the change in the product itself. Statisticians use methods intended to separate price change from quality change, but the distinction can be technically difficult.

Housing creates special challenges because buying a home involves both consumption and investment. Different indexes handle housing costs in different ways, so two measures can move differently even when both are carefully constructed.

These complications do not make price indexes arbitrary. Transparent methods allow users to understand what each index is designed to measure. Problems arise when one index is treated as a perfect measure of every household’s cost of living.

For policy and personal decisions, the right question is often not “What is the true inflation rate?” but “Which index best matches the population, spending pattern, and purpose relevant to this decision?”

Model answer (104 words)
Activities that mainly deliver information can move online, while activities whose value depends on immediate interaction should remain face to face. A large lecture explaining established concepts could be streamed because students can receive the same content without traveling to campus. A laboratory, clinical exercise, or small discussion should stay in person because students must respond to equipment or other people in real time. The university should therefore classify activities by what students must actually do, not by course title. This principle keeps the flexibility of online teaching while protecting forms of learning that lose important information or practice when moved to a screen.

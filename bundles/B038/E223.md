Part 1｜Reading A

Modern systems are often judged by benchmarks. A benchmark gives people a common task, dataset, or measurement procedure so that different systems can be compared under similar conditions. In medicine, a diagnostic method may be evaluated on a shared set of cases. In computing, programs may be tested on the same collection of tasks. In education, students may take a standardized examination designed to measure particular skills.

Benchmarks are useful because comparison without a common reference can become vague. If one team reports that its method is “good” and another makes the same claim, the statements cannot easily be compared unless the teams have used similar standards. A benchmark turns an abstract judgment into a measurable contest.

However, a benchmark is not identical to the real-world goal. It is a sample or proxy chosen to represent that goal. The cases included in the benchmark may come from a particular period, population, or technological environment. Performance can therefore improve even while the connection between the benchmark and the broader goal becomes weaker.

One cause is repeated optimization. Once a benchmark becomes important, researchers study it closely. They learn which types of cases are common, which errors affect the score most, and which design choices produce higher results. This effort is rational: the benchmark was created precisely to guide improvement. Yet the more a system is adapted to a fixed benchmark, the more likely it is that some gains reflect knowledge of the benchmark rather than a general improvement.

[ A ]

This problem does not require deliberate cheating. Suppose a benchmark contains many examples from a small set of sources. A team may discover that a feature works especially well on those sources and incorporate it into the system. The score rises. If future real-world cases come from different sources, however, the same feature may provide little benefit.

Benchmarks can also age. A test of online behavior created five years ago may no longer reflect current devices, habits, or security threats. A medical dataset collected at one hospital may not represent patients seen elsewhere after treatment practices change. A language test may emphasize tasks that were once difficult but have become routine.

For these reasons, strong evaluation uses more than one kind of evidence. Researchers may keep part of a dataset hidden until final testing, rotate benchmark tasks, create new challenge sets, or test systems in settings that differ from those used during development. They may also report several metrics rather than a single score.

A benchmark should therefore be treated as a tool for disciplined comparison, not as the goal itself. High performance is meaningful when the benchmark continues to represent the ability that matters. The critical question is not simply whether the score increased, but whether the system became better at the broader task the score was intended to approximate.

Part 2｜Reading B

Risk information is difficult to communicate because numbers that are mathematically equivalent can produce different impressions. A treatment described as having a 90 percent survival rate may sound more reassuring than one described as having a 10 percent mortality rate, even though the two statements refer to the same outcome.

The problem becomes greater when relative changes are reported without a baseline. Imagine that a certain event occurs in two out of every thousand people in one group and in one out of every thousand people in another. The second group has half the risk, so the relative reduction is 50 percent. But the absolute reduction is one case per thousand people.

Neither description is necessarily wrong. Relative risk can help reveal the strength of an association, while absolute risk helps show the practical size of the difference. Misunderstanding occurs when one number is presented as if it answered both questions.

Frequency formats can sometimes make probabilities easier to interpret. Saying that “2 out of 1,000 people” experienced an event may be more concrete than giving a percentage with several decimal places. The best format depends on what comparison the audience needs to make.

[ B ]

Uncertainty must also be communicated. A study does not usually reveal one perfectly known number. Its estimate may vary because only a sample was observed, because outcomes were measured imperfectly, or because future patients differ from those in the study.

Communicating uncertainty does not mean making every decision impossible. People often need to act before all uncertainty disappears. The aim is to distinguish what is known with reasonable confidence from what remains uncertain and to show which uncertainties could change the decision.

Visual displays can help when they preserve the denominator and make alternatives comparable. A graph showing 1,000 small icons, for example, can display how many people experience each outcome under two options. But a dramatic color scale or truncated axis can exaggerate small differences.

Effective risk communication therefore requires more than accurate arithmetic. It requires choosing a representation that answers the decision maker’s question without hiding the baseline, the comparison group, or the uncertainty surrounding the estimate.

Model answer (105 words)

I think grade distributions should be available to students, but only with enough context to prevent simple comparisons between courses. The data could help students understand whether a low score reflects unusually difficult material or a personal weakness that needs attention. However, students might choose courses only because past grades were high. For example, a demanding but valuable class could lose enrollment if its distribution is viewed without information about course goals and assessment methods. Universities should therefore publish grade distributions together with descriptions of assignments and grading policies. Transparency would then support informed choices without turning grades into the only measure of course quality.

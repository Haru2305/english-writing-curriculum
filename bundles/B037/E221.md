Part 1｜Reading A

Organizations often invite users to express opinions through surveys, comment forms, public meetings, or online consultations. Large numbers of responses can create a strong impression that the results show what the community wants.

The difficulty is that participation is rarely random. People who feel strongly about an issue may be more likely to answer. A service change that causes serious inconvenience for a small group can motivate many responses from that group, while satisfied users may remain silent.

This does not make the responses unimportant. Strongly affected users may reveal problems that average usage statistics miss. The mistake is treating the set of respondents as if it were automatically a miniature version of the entire population.

[ A ]

Representativeness depends on who had an opportunity to participate and who chose to do so. An online survey may underrepresent people who rarely use the website. A meeting held during working hours may exclude people who cannot attend then. A survey offered only in one language may narrow participation further.

Researchers can compare respondent characteristics with information about the larger population. If one age group, neighborhood, or user type is heavily overrepresented, the result can be interpreted with that imbalance in mind.

Weighting responses can sometimes adjust known differences, but it cannot fully correct for characteristics that were never measured. Nor can it reveal the views of people who would not respond under any version of the survey.

Public consultation is therefore best treated as one source of evidence. It can identify experiences, arguments, and concerns, but decisions should also consider usage data, targeted outreach, and the distribution of people who did not participate.

Part 2｜Reading B

Predictive models are built by finding patterns in existing data. A model may use past observations to estimate future demand, classify images, or identify people who are likely to respond to a treatment.

A model that matches its training data very closely can appear impressive. Yet excellent performance on familiar data does not guarantee that the same model will work well on new cases.

The reason is that data contain both stable relationships and accidental detail. If a model is flexible enough, it may learn small irregularities that happened to occur in the training sample. Those irregularities need not appear again.

This problem is called overfitting. It is especially tempting when developers repeatedly adjust a model after examining its errors on the same data. Each adjustment can make the observed score look better while making the model more dependent on that particular sample.

[ B ]

A common response is to reserve data that are not used during training. The model is developed without access to these cases and is then evaluated on them. Performance on the reserved data gives a better estimate of how the model handles unfamiliar examples.

Even this does not answer every question. If the reserved data come from the same time, place, or population as the training data, success may not guarantee performance after conditions change.

Researchers therefore distinguish internal validation from broader generalization. They may test the model in a later period, another institution, or a group with different characteristics. Each new setting asks whether the learned relationship remains useful outside the environment that produced it.

Model answer (105 words)
The university should guarantee access to campus housing but not require every first-year student to live there. Instead, all first-year students should join a small orientation group that meets several times during the first semester. This supports social connection directly rather than using residence as a rough substitute for it. A commuter who lives with family could join the same group activities as students in dormitories without paying for unnecessary housing. Students with caregiving, financial, or accessibility constraints should also be allowed alternatives. The policy would preserve opportunities to build community while recognizing that the same living arrangement creates very different costs for different students.

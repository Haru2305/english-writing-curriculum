Part 1｜Reading A

Archives are often treated as collections of evidence about the past. Letters, official reports, newspapers, photographs, financial records, and personal diaries can allow later researchers to reconstruct events that no living observer can remember.

Yet archives do not contain a neutral sample of everything that happened. Records must first be created, then preserved, organized, and made accessible. At each stage, some experiences are more likely to survive than others.

Institutions with money and administrative systems often leave large quantities of written material. People with less access to formal organizations may appear less often in the record, even when their experiences were socially important.

The content of records is selective as well. An official report may document what an institution considered relevant, not everything that affected the people involved. A personal diary may describe private experiences but omit events the writer regarded as ordinary.

[ A ]

For this reason, absence in an archive is difficult to interpret. If a practice is rarely mentioned, it may have been uncommon. But it may also have been considered too ordinary to record, too sensitive to preserve, or associated with people whose records were not collected.

Historians therefore ask questions about the production of evidence itself. Who created the document? For what purpose? What kinds of information were expected to be written down? What may have been excluded before the archive was formed?

Researchers can also combine different kinds of sources. Administrative records, oral histories, material objects, and newspapers may reveal different aspects of the same period. Agreement among them can strengthen an interpretation, while disagreement can expose the limits of a particular source.

The archive is thus both evidence and a product of historical processes. Understanding what survives requires attention not only to the documents that are present but also to the systems that determined what could be recorded and preserved.

Part 2｜Reading B

Scientific results can be checked in more than one way. Two terms that are sometimes confused are reproducibility and replication.

Reproducibility generally asks whether the reported result can be obtained again using the same data and the same or closely specified analytical procedure. If another researcher receives the dataset and code, can the calculation be repeated?

Replication asks a different question. Researchers collect new data and test whether a similar pattern appears again. A study may therefore be reproducible without being successfully replicated.

This distinction matters because the two checks detect different problems. Failure of reproducibility may reveal missing code, unclear procedures, data errors, or analytical steps that were not fully documented.

A failed replication can have other explanations. The original result may have depended on a particular population, time period, environment, or measurement method. The new study may differ in a feature that changes the effect.

[ B ]

For this reason, replication is not simply a vote in which one study cancels another. Researchers compare methods, samples, uncertainty, and the conditions under which each result was obtained.

Repeated evidence across different settings can increase confidence that a finding is broadly applicable. At the same time, variation across settings can define the boundaries of that applicability.

Good scientific evaluation therefore asks both whether a result can be reconstructed from the original evidence and whether it survives contact with new evidence.

Model answer (104 words)
The university should require one course outside a student's main field during the first two years and let students choose from a broad list. The purpose should be to practice a different way of asking questions, not simply to accumulate unrelated credits. For example, a science student might take ethics and examine how evidence, values, and social consequences interact. One course is enough to create that exposure without taking substantial time from specialized study. Departments should also approve several options that connect naturally with their own fields. This structure makes breadth a deliberate learning goal while leaving most of the curriculum available for depth.

Part 1｜Reading A

Preventive work is difficult to value because its success is often invisible. When a bridge is repaired before a serious defect develops, commuters simply continue to cross it. When software is updated before a security failure occurs, users may notice nothing at all. The absence of disruption is the intended result.

This creates a problem for organizations deciding how to spend limited resources. A visible breakdown produces clear evidence: trains stop, a building closes, or a computer system fails. Preventive maintenance produces a counterfactual claim. It asks decision makers to believe that a problem would have occurred if maintenance had not been performed.

For this reason, counting failures before and after maintenance is not always enough. A system may become older, receive heavier use, or face more extreme conditions over time. If the number of failures remains constant despite greater risk, maintenance may still have been effective.

[ A ]

Comparison is also complicated by selection. Equipment judged to be at high risk may receive more inspection and repair. If researchers later observe that heavily maintained equipment still fails more often than lightly maintained equipment, they cannot simply conclude that maintenance caused the failures. The heavily maintained equipment may have been more vulnerable from the beginning.

One approach is to compare similar units that differ mainly in the timing or intensity of maintenance. Another is to examine changes within the same unit over time while accounting for age, use, and environmental conditions. Neither method perfectly recreates the world in which no maintenance occurred, but both can make the comparison more informative.

The broader lesson is that prevention should not be evaluated only by visible events. Good evaluation asks what risk existed before the intervention, what changed after it, and what would plausibly have happened without it.

Part 2｜Reading B

Students often judge a study method by how easy learning feels while they are using it. Reading the same page several times can create strong familiarity. The words become easier to recognize, and the learner may feel that the material has been mastered.

Familiarity, however, is not the same as being able to retrieve information later. A student who recognizes an answer while looking at notes may be unable to produce it during an examination when the notes are absent.

Retrieval practice addresses this gap by requiring the learner to bring information to mind. The attempt itself matters. Even an unsuccessful attempt can reveal what has been forgotten and make later feedback more useful.

Timing changes the difficulty of retrieval. If practice occurs immediately after learning, the answer may still be active in short-term memory. If practice occurs much later, the learner may remember too little to retrieve the information successfully.

Researchers therefore study spacing: how long to wait before a learner is asked to recall something again. The most useful interval depends on the desired retention period, the complexity of the material, and the learner’s current knowledge.

[ B ]

This means that a study method should not be evaluated only by performance during the study session. A method that produces slightly more errors today may lead to better recall a week later. The relevant test should therefore resemble the future use of the knowledge in both timing and required response.

Spacing is not a rule that every interval must be long. Its purpose is to create enough forgetting that retrieval requires effort without making successful recall impossible. The appropriate difficulty is productive, not maximal.

Part 1｜Reading A

Peer review asks students to evaluate work produced by other students rather than relying only on comments from an instructor. It can increase the amount of feedback available and can also teach reviewers to notice strengths and weaknesses that they may later recognize in their own writing.

The social relationship between reviewer and writer, however, affects what feedback is given. A student may hesitate to criticize a close friend or may soften a comment because the writer will know who wrote it. In other cases, a reviewer may be influenced by reputation before reading the work carefully.

Anonymity is one possible response. If the writer does not know the reviewer’s identity, the reviewer may feel freer to describe a problem directly. If the reviewer also does not know the writer’s identity, judgments may depend less on friendship, status, or prior performance.

[ A ]

Yet anonymity can weaken another useful force: accountability. A named reviewer knows that an unhelpful comment can be traced back to them. An anonymous reviewer may be more willing to write a vague or harsh reaction without explaining it.

The quality of peer review therefore depends on task design as much as on identity. A prompt such as “Give feedback” provides little guidance. A prompt that asks reviewers to identify one claim, explain one difficulty, and propose one revision makes the expected reasoning clearer.

Teachers can also separate visibility from accountability. Reviews may be anonymous between students while the instructor can still identify the author of each review. This arrangement protects students from some social pressure without making comments completely untraceable.

The broader lesson is that anonymity is not simply present or absent. Different participants can have different levels of access to identity, and those choices should be matched to the purpose of the feedback process.

Part 2｜Reading B

A screening test is often judged by how well it distinguishes people who have a target condition from those who do not. Two kinds of error are especially important. A false negative occurs when the condition is present but the test is negative. A false positive occurs when the condition is absent but the test is positive.

It is tempting to think that a highly accurate test makes a positive result almost certain to be correct. That conclusion can fail when the target condition is rare in the population being tested.

Imagine a condition present in only a small fraction of a large group. Even if the test correctly identifies most people with the condition, there are very few true cases available to produce true positive results. Meanwhile, the much larger group without the condition can produce some false positives.

As a result, the number of false positives may be substantial relative to the number of true positives. The test has not necessarily performed badly. The composition of the tested population affects what a positive result means.

[ B ]

This is why test characteristics and population frequency answer different questions. Sensitivity and specificity describe aspects of test performance. The probability that a particular positive result represents the condition also depends on how common the condition was before testing.

A second test can sometimes provide additional evidence because it changes the information available after the first result. The purpose is not to “correct” every positive result automatically, but to update the judgment with more evidence.

The general principle extends beyond health screening. Fraud detection, quality control, and security alerts can all produce many false alarms when the event being detected is rare. Interpretation requires both the performance of the detector and the frequency of the event.

Model answer (98 words)

I think universities should make student peer review partly anonymous, especially when classmates are giving critical feedback on early drafts. Anonymity can reduce the pressure to protect friendships and make students more willing to point out unclear reasoning. However, anonymous comments can also become careless. For example, a student might write “This makes no sense” without explaining why. Teachers should therefore require reviewers to identify one specific problem and suggest one possible improvement. The teacher should also be able to see who wrote each review even if students cannot. This system would protect honest feedback while preserving responsibility.

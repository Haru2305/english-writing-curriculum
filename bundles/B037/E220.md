Part 1｜Reading A

Rules are often introduced to make decisions consistent. A university may set one deadline for all students, a hospital may use a standard triage procedure, and a company may require the same documentation for every expense. Consistency can make outcomes easier to predict and can reduce the influence of personal preference.

Yet identical treatment is not always equivalent to fair treatment. Two people may face different circumstances that are relevant to the decision. A student who submits an assignment late because of poor planning is not in the same situation as a student whose submission was delayed by an unexpected hospitalization.

Allowing exceptions appears to solve this problem, but discretion creates a second difficulty. If decision makers can depart from the rule whenever they judge a case unusual, similar cases may receive different outcomes depending on who reviews them.

[ A ]

The challenge is therefore not simply to choose between rules and discretion. Institutions need a procedure for deciding when deviation is justified. That procedure may define categories of acceptable evidence, specify who can approve an exception, and record the reason for the decision.

Documentation serves two purposes. First, it allows later reviewers to check whether similar cases were treated similarly. Second, it reveals when a rule itself is producing repeated problems. If many exceptions arise for the same reason, changing the general rule may be more sensible than continuing to make individual adjustments.

Transparency also has limits. Publishing every detail of a case can threaten privacy. A fair process may need to make the criteria and reasoning structure visible without exposing sensitive personal information.

A strong policy therefore combines a stable default with a controlled route for exceptions. The goal is not to make every outcome identical but to make relevant differences explicit and decisions explainable.

Part 2｜Reading B

Ecologists often survey a site to determine which species are present. At first glance, the task seems simple: observe the site, record the organisms found, and compare the list with another location or time.

The difficulty is that a species can be present without being detected. Some animals are active only at certain hours. Others are quiet during bad weather, hidden during parts of the year, or difficult to distinguish from similar species.

Detection also depends on the observer and method. A bird identified by an experienced listener may be missed by someone relying only on visual observation. A camera trap may detect nocturnal mammals that a daytime survey would rarely record.

A single failure to observe a species therefore provides weaker evidence than a confirmed observation. Presence can often be established by one reliable detection, while absence requires stronger support.

[ B ]

Repeated surveys help because they create multiple opportunities for detection. If a species that is usually easy to observe is missed many times under suitable conditions, true absence becomes more plausible. If the species is difficult to detect, the same sequence of negative surveys provides weaker evidence.

Researchers can also model detection probability using information about weather, season, survey duration, equipment, and observer experience. The goal is not to produce certainty but to separate two processes: whether the species occupies the site and whether the survey detects it when present.

This distinction matters when evaluating environmental change. A lower number of observed species may reflect real decline, poorer survey conditions, or both. Without considering detection, apparent change can be confused with change in observation quality.

Model answer (97 words)

I think group projects should include an individual part in the final grade. Teamwork is useful because students must divide tasks, explain ideas, and respond to other people. However, one shared grade can hide large differences in contribution. For example, a student who completes most of a report may receive the same score as a classmate who does very little. Teachers could combine a group score with a short individual reflection or oral explanation. This would keep the benefits of collaboration while giving teachers more evidence about what each student actually understood and contributed to the project.

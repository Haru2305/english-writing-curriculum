Part 1｜Reading A

Maps appear to show places as they are, but every map is a selective model. A map cannot display every building, path, elevation change, land use, administrative boundary, and temporary condition at the same level of detail. To be usable, it must emphasize some features and reduce or omit others.

The choices depend on purpose. A subway map may distort distance while preserving station order and connections. A hiking map may show elevation in detail but ignore many commercial buildings. A political map may emphasize administrative borders that are irrelevant to someone planning a walking route.

This selectivity does not make maps inaccurate in a simple sense. A map can be highly useful even when some spatial relationships are intentionally simplified. The question is whether the simplification preserves the information needed for the task.

[ A ]

Boundaries deserve special attention. A line on a map can appear exact even when the phenomenon it represents changes gradually. A flood-risk zone, for example, may be drawn with a clear border although actual risk changes continuously with elevation, drainage, rainfall, and building design.

The apparent precision of the line can influence decisions. A property just outside a marked zone may seem completely safe, while one just inside may seem categorically dangerous. In reality, the difference between the two locations may be small.

Good map use therefore requires users to ask what the map was designed to support, how its categories were created, and which uncertainties are hidden by a clean visual boundary.

Part 2｜Reading B

Cities increasingly use networks of sensors to study local temperature. The goal may be to identify neighborhoods exposed to high heat, evaluate tree-planting programs, or guide decisions about cooling centers.

Temperature, however, can vary substantially over short distances. A sensor above dark pavement may record a different pattern from one near trees or water. Height, shade, airflow, nearby walls, and the time of day can all influence the reading.

This means that a sensor does not simply measure “the temperature of the neighborhood.” It measures temperature at a particular place, height, and moment. Researchers must decide how far that observation can reasonably represent surrounding conditions.

Adding more sensors can improve coverage, but quantity alone does not solve the problem. If most sensors are placed where installation is convenient, such as on public buildings with reliable power, the network may still miss locations where people experience the greatest heat.

[ B ]

Standardization can also improve comparison. When sensors are installed at similar heights and protected from direct sunlight in consistent ways, differences among locations are easier to interpret as environmental rather than procedural.

At the same time, perfectly uniform placement is not always the goal. Researchers may deliberately place some sensors in unusual environments to understand variation. What matters is that the purpose and conditions of each measurement are documented.

A useful heat map therefore combines measurements with information about where and how they were collected. The map is strongest when users can distinguish observed data from interpolation and understand where uncertainty remains high.

Model answer (100 words)

I think universities should make lecture recordings available after class, but they should not treat recordings as a replacement for attending. Recordings help students review difficult explanations and are especially useful when illness or unavoidable problems prevent attendance. However, some students may postpone watching them and fall behind. For example, a student could miss several lectures and plan to watch everything before an exam. Universities should therefore release recordings together with short deadlines or follow-up quizzes that encourage regular study. This would preserve the flexibility of recorded lectures while still giving students a reason to keep pace with the course.

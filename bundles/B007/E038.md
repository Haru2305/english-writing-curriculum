Reading
A secondary school is considering whether to start classes later in the morning. Some students say they are tired during the first lesson, while teachers worry that a later finish could affect clubs, transport, and family schedules.


A teacher might suggest testing a later start for one month. This is a relatively open idea that invites discussion. If the school leadership develops a detailed timetable, transport plan, and trial period, it may formally propose a new schedule to the school board.


A health professional may recommend a later start if evidence suggests that students are not getting enough sleep and if the expected benefits outweigh practical disadvantages. The recommendation is stronger because it is presented as the preferred option based on relevant evidence.


The three verbs can therefore describe different stages of the same debate. Someone can suggest an idea before details are fixed, propose a concrete plan for decision, and recommend one option after comparing evidence and alternatives. The distinction becomes useful when several stakeholders speak at once. Students may suggest practical changes, administrators may propose a timetable that can actually be implemented, and specialists may recommend one option because of evidence about sleep or learning. These roles can overlap, but the wording helps show whether a speaker is offering an idea, presenting a plan, or giving advice.


Good decision making also requires feedback. A pilot can measure attendance, punctuality, sleep, transport problems, and participation in after-school activities. It may also reveal effects that were not obvious during planning, such as problems for families with younger children or changes in bus demand. The school can then revise the proposal rather than assuming that the first design is final.

Reading
[1] A city is considering whether to use public money to host a large international sports event. Supporters argue that the event could attract visitors, create a shared sense of excitement, and accelerate improvements to transportation or public spaces. Opponents answer that stadiums, security, temporary facilities, and later maintenance can place a heavy burden on public budgets. Some also question whether the short period of international attention would justify years of preparation. If the debate stops at these two lists, however, it is difficult to know what should count as success. The same project can look attractive or wasteful depending on which effects are counted and what alternatives are ignored.


[2] One reason is opportunity cost. Money used for an event cannot be used at the same time for schools, health services, local sports programs, or other public projects. Yet this does not mean that every event-related expense is wasted. A transport line built partly for visitors may continue to serve residents for years, and a renovated public space may have uses that extend beyond the event itself. The relevant comparison is therefore not simply “spend” versus “do not spend,” but one use of limited resources versus realistic alternatives. A lower-cost project that produces smaller benefits may sometimes be a better choice if it leaves room for other urgent needs.


[3] A second issue is distribution. Even when total economic activity increases, the benefits and burdens may not be shared equally. Hotels and restaurants near event sites may gain more than businesses elsewhere. Residents near venues may experience crowding or noise, while taxpayers across the city share the cost. Workers may gain temporary jobs without receiving the same long-term benefits as property owners or major contractors. This makes a single headline number less informative than it first appears. A project can produce a net benefit overall and still raise difficult questions about who receives that benefit and who bears the risks.


[4] The central question, then, is not whether large sports events are always good or always bad. It is whether a particular proposal can show additional benefits that justify its public cost when compared with other options. Useful criteria include the likely long-term use of new facilities, the groups expected to benefit, the costs that remain after the event, and the opportunity cost of alternative projects. Decision makers should also distinguish benefits that would have occurred anyway from those genuinely created by the event. Clear criteria do not eliminate disagreement, but they make the disagreement more precise. Instead of arguing only from enthusiasm or fear, citizens can ask which assumptions actually determine the conclusion.

Reading answers
Q1｜B
Paragraph 2の仕事は「費用がある」と言うことではなく、比較軸をspend / do not spendから、one use of limited resources / realistic alternativesへ変えること。


Q2｜C
本文は、同じ支出でも代替案と比べなければ価値を判断しにくいと述べる。AはParagraph 3が否定、BとDは本文にない。


Q3｜C
A・B・Dはいずれも一部の論点だけを文章全体の結論へ拡大している。CだけがParagraph 2〜4のopportunity cost・distribution・long-term useをまとめる。


Q4｜解答例
The author argues that a sports-event proposal should be evaluated against realistic alternatives rather than accepted or rejected in general. Important criteria include opportunity cost, the distribution of benefits and burdens, and the long-term use of facilities.


A06｜主旨・要約
主旨は「一番重要そうな名詞」を拾う作業ではない。E055で取った段落機能を並べ、全段落を最小の命題で説明する。
P1 debate → P2 alternative comparison → P3 distribution → P4 criteria
この流れをまとめると、「特定の大会を明示的な基準と代替案比較で評価すべき」が中心になる。


RQ08｜主旨問題
誤答は「本文中に書いてある」ことが多い。見るべきは、その選択肢が全文を説明できるか。
具体例だけ、片方の立場だけ、結論より強すぎる断定は落とす。

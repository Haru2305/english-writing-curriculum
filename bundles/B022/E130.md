Reading

[1] A farming cooperative wanted to reduce the amount of edible produce discarded because it did not meet cosmetic standards. Its first question was whether lower prices would be enough to create demand for irregularly shaped vegetables. The cooperative began selling such produce at a discount in several local shops.



[2] Sales increased at first, suggesting that price mattered. Yet the effect was uneven. Some customers were happy to buy the discounted vegetables, while others avoided them because they were unsure how long the produce would keep or whether unusual shapes indicated lower quality. The cooperative realized that a lower price answered only part of the problem.



[3] Packaging information changed purchasing behavior more than expected. When labels explained that shape did not affect safety or taste and gave clear storage advice, more customers were willing to try the products. Recipe cards also helped because some items were unfamiliar in size or appearance. These additions did not reduce the price further; they reduced uncertainty.



[4] Another issue appeared upstream. Farmers sometimes delivered large quantities of irregular produce at the same time, but shops had limited space. Even if customers were willing to buy the products, sudden oversupply still led to waste. The cooperative therefore began sharing weekly forecasts with shops and arranging smaller, more frequent deliveries. This change made the program less visible to customers than a large discount sign, but it addressed waste before the produce reached the shelf.



[5] The shops also learned that placement mattered. When irregular produce was separated in a distant bargain area, some customers treated it as damaged goods. When it was displayed beside standard produce with a clear explanation, shoppers could compare the options directly. The design of the choice therefore affected how the discount was interpreted.



[6] The program suggests that the key question is not simply how to make imperfect-looking produce cheaper. The broader question is how to connect supply with consumers who understand the product and can use it. Price can attract attention, but information and distribution determine whether that interest becomes a stable market.



[7] The cooperative did not eliminate waste, and the program would not work equally well for every crop or region. Still, the trial showed why a single-variable answer can be misleading. A practical response to food waste may require several linked changes rather than one dramatic intervention.

解答・解説・自己修正



Reading answers

Q1｜解答例

It changes from whether lower prices are enough to whether supply can be connected with consumers through price, information, and distribution together.



Q2｜解答例

Reducing waste from irregular produce requires more than discounting; uncertainty, product presentation, and supply coordination also shape whether demand becomes stable.



Q3｜解答例

Labels, storage advice, and recipe guidance can be compressed as information, while forecasts, delivery frequency, and shop capacity can be compressed as distribution.
補足：陳列（標準品の隣に説明付きで置く）は、information（説明）とも distribution（置き場所）とも整理できる。

Model summary

規格外農産物の廃棄を減らすため、生産者の組合はまず値下げを試し、売上は伸びたが、品質や日持ちへの不安から買わない客もいた。表示や保存方法の説明、レシピ、陳列の工夫で不安を減らし、出荷予測の共有や小口配送で供給の偏りも抑えた。課題は価格だけでなく、消費者の理解と供給を結ぶ仕組みにあり、単一の対策では不十分だと分かった。

Reading

[1] A city renovated a small public square beside a railway station. The project added benches, trees, and space for evening events. After the renovation, average daytime sound levels were slightly lower than before because buses no longer stopped directly beside the square. Yet complaints about noise increased. Why would residents report more disturbance when the average level had fallen?



[2] One possible answer is that averages hide short peaks. Before the renovation, traffic created a fairly constant background sound. Afterward, most periods were quieter, but deliveries, outdoor performances, and groups leaving nearby restaurants sometimes produced brief bursts of much louder sound. A single average number can therefore describe the total sound exposure without showing when the loudest moments occur. If officials ask only whether the average rose or fell, they may miss the feature that residents actually notice.



[3] Timing also matters. A five-minute burst in the afternoon may attract little attention, while the same burst late at night can interrupt sleep. Interviews with residents suggested that complaints were concentrated after 10 p.m. This pattern does not prove that every late event caused serious harm, but it shows that the question cannot be answered by comparing daytime averages alone. Measurement must match the experience the policy is meant to improve.



[4] Predictability may be another part of the answer. People often adapt to regular background sounds, such as a train that passes at known times. Irregular sounds can be harder to ignore because residents do not know when they will begin or how long they will continue. In the square, scheduled events produced fewer complaints than unscheduled late gatherings of similar measured intensity. Residents also reported that advance notice made planned events easier to tolerate.



[5] Complaints themselves should not be treated as a perfect measure of noise. People differ in sensitivity, and a change in the neighborhood may make residents more willing to report problems. Still, the complaints are useful because they reveal a mismatch between the city’s original measure and the lived pattern of disturbance.



[6] The increase in complaints therefore does not mean that the renovation simply made the area louder. The better answer is that disturbance depends on the pattern and context of sound, not only on its average level. To evaluate the problem, the city needs information about peaks, timing, duration, and predictability. The original question—why complaints rose while the average fell—can be answered only after separating these dimensions.

Q3｜解答例

It might conclude that the renovation succeeded and dismiss the complaints, missing the late-night peaks and unpredictable sounds that residents actually notice ([2], [3]).

Noise disturbance depends on when and how sound occurs, not only on its average level, so evaluation must match the actual pattern residents experience.

Model output

The passage asks why complaints increased even though average sound levels fell. The answer is that average volume does not capture the full experience of noise. Short peaks, late timing, and irregular or unpredictable sounds may be more disturbing than a steady background level. Complaints therefore reflect the pattern and context of sound as well as its measured average.

Reading
[1] A city is considering how to improve access to a small network of public study rooms. The rooms are free, but opening hours differ by location. A recent survey suggests that some potential users are blocked by schedule rather than by lack of interest.
[2] The city has enough funding for only one pilot change. It wants the pilot to do three things: address a documented barrier, reach people who currently use the rooms infrequently, and produce interpretable evidence within two months. A change that merely increases convenience for frequent users is useful but lower priority for this pilot.
[3] Three options have been proposed. Option A extends one central room by two evening hours. Option B opens a smaller neighborhood room on Sunday. Option C adds an online reservation system to all rooms. The table summarizes current use and survey responses.
[4] The figures should not be read mechanically. A high percentage saying that a change would help does not prove that all those people will use it. Current use also matters: a location with already-high use may show convenience demand rather than unmet access. The pilot should therefore combine stated benefit with current-use patterns and the specific barrier each option addresses.
[5] After the pilot, the city should compare actual new users with the survey prediction. If the change attracts mostly existing frequent users, the program may have improved convenience without substantially improving reach. That result would still be informative because it would show that the original interpretation of the barrier needs revision.

Reading answers
Q1
Option B. It targets a reported schedule barrier among low-frequency users and can be tested within a clear two-month pilot.
Q2｜解答例
Because reported benefit is an intention measure rather than observed future behavior, and high interest among frequent users may reflect convenience rather than newly created access.
Q3｜解答例
The claim is cautious. The author treats survey interest as useful evidence but explicitly says it does not prove actual future use.
Model summary
The city should choose a study-room pilot by combining current use, reported barriers, and expected benefit rather than following one survey percentage. Its priority is to improve access for infrequent users and generate clear evidence. After the pilot, actual new-user behavior should be compared with survey expectations to test whether the original barrier was identified correctly.

Model answer
The city should prioritize Option B because the pilot should reduce a documented access barrier and reach infrequent users. The table shows that the neighborhood room has low current use but strong reported Sunday benefit, which is more consistent with unmet schedule access than Option A’s already-busy central room. The main uncertainty is that survey interest may not become actual attendance. The city should therefore run the Sunday opening for two months and compare new-user numbers, repeat use, and actual attendance with the survey prediction. This would make the final funding choice more evidence-based.

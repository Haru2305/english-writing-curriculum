Passage
[1] Can observations collected by volunteers contribute to scientific research? A simple answer is yes: large numbers of participants can gather information across places and times that a small research team could not cover alone. Yet volume alone does not guarantee useful evidence.

[2] Early versions of a fictional bird-monitoring project accepted any submitted sighting with a location and species name. This produced a large database, but researchers later discovered that some patterns reflected observer behavior as much as bird behavior. Popular parks had many reports, while less visited areas appeared unusually empty.

[3] The project therefore changed its protocol. Volunteers were asked to record not only what they saw but also how long they observed, how far they traveled, and whether they reported every species detected rather than only unusual birds. These additional fields did not eliminate bias, but they made effort more visible.

[4] The revised method improved interpretation. If two sites had different numbers of reported birds, researchers could ask whether the difference remained after considering observation time and reporting completeness. In other words, the project moved from counting sightings alone toward modeling both the animals and the process that produced the records.

[5] An exception remained. Rare-species reports were often submitted individually rather than as complete lists because participants were especially motivated to report unusual observations. Those records were still valuable for locating possible rare species, but they were less suitable for estimating ordinary abundance.

[6] The project now treats different record types as useful for different questions. Complete observation lists can support comparisons of relative frequency when observer effort is modeled. Isolated rare-species reports can help identify locations for follow-up, but they should not be mixed into abundance estimates without additional controls.

Protocol Note
A “complete list” means the observer reports all bird species detected during a defined observation period, not only the species of special interest.

解答・解説・自己修正

Q1｜解答例
The passage asks whether volunteer observations can contribute to research. Its main answer is yes, but only when researchers account for how the observations were produced and match record types to appropriate questions.

Q2｜解答例
The early system mainly counted submitted sightings. The revised system also records observation effort and reporting completeness, allowing researchers to model the observation process as well as the birds.

Q3｜解答例
Rare-species reports are often submitted outside complete observation lists and lack comparable effort information, so they are less suitable for estimating ordinary abundance.

Q4｜解答例
Complete lists, because effort is recorded and reporting completeness is high, making comparisons more interpretable when effort is modeled.

Q5｜解答例
The passage is explicitly qualified. It treats volunteer data as useful for some purposes but repeatedly notes bias, incomplete reporting, and differences among record types.

Model summary
Volunteer observations can expand scientific coverage, but large record numbers alone do not guarantee reliable evidence. The project improved its early sighting database by recording observation effort and whether volunteers submitted complete species lists. This allows researchers to model the reporting process as well as bird occurrence. However, isolated rare-species reports often lack comparable effort data. Complete lists therefore best support relative-frequency comparisons, while rare-species reports are more useful for locating candidates for follow-up.

Model evaluation
A research team should use volunteer observations for abundance research only when the records include comparable information about observation effort and reporting completeness. Complete lists are useful because researchers can adjust comparisons for time and search effort, while the large number of observations expands geographic coverage. However, partial lists and isolated rare-species reports should not be mixed directly into abundance estimates because they are produced by different reporting behavior. The team should therefore base abundance analyses mainly on complete lists and use other record types for presence evidence or follow-up locations.

## Checkpoint 解説ルート

このセットの中心は **research question → data-generating process → usable evidence → exception → fit-for-purpose decision**。

1. **Question**: volunteer data は研究に使えるか
2. **Data-generating process**: sightings は鳥の存在だけでなく、where / how long / what people choose to report によって生まれる
3. **Revision**: effort と completeness を記録して観察過程を可視化する
4. **Exception**: rare-species reports は報告動機が異なり、abundance estimation にそのまま混ぜられない
5. **Decision**: record type を research question に合わせる

### 弱い考え方との比較
× 「18,400件あるので信頼できる」  
○ 「件数が多くても、観察 effort と reporting process が偏れば abundance は歪む。complete lists のように生成過程を比較しやすい記録を使う」  
改善点は、**volume と validity を分ける**こと。

### 答案の組み立て
Model evaluation は、conditional Position → criteria（effort / completeness）→ mechanism（比較可能性）→ benefit（coverage）→ qualification（partial / rare reports）→ fit-for-purpose use、の順で進む。Record type ごとに用途を変えることが結論であり、「volunteer data を使う / 使わない」の二択ではない。

### 次の問題に持っていくもの
データの価値は件数だけでなく、**どう生成されたか、欠け方が比較可能か、目的と測定が合っているか**で決める。

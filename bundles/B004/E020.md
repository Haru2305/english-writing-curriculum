Reading
A local government has a limited budget for public services. It must decide how much money to allocate to transport, libraries, clinics, parks, and maintenance. Spending more in one area usually means having less available for another.


Population decline can make this problem harder. The number of users may fall, but the fixed cost of keeping a building, route, or service open can remain high. Closing a service may reduce spending, yet it can also make access worse for people who depend on it.


Budget decisions therefore involve more than comparing how many people use each service. Officials may allocate funds to maintain an essential route with relatively few users while reducing spending somewhere else. They may also assign staff differently so that one worker can support several nearby facilities.


Emergency support creates a different task. After a storm, the government may need to distribute supplies among several communities. The question is no longer which long-term program receives funding, but how physical resources should reach people quickly and fairly.


Good budgeting requires clear priorities. A government cannot fund every service at the highest level, so it must explain what it values, what alternatives exist, and who will be affected by each choice. Equal funding is not always equal treatment. A small rural clinic may need more funding per patient than a large urban facility because transport, staffing, or maintenance costs are different. At the same time, giving one service more money creates an opportunity cost elsewhere. Transparent criteria can help residents understand why resources are allocated differently even when they disagree with the final decision.

## Core recap

### 核
予算配分は **resource limit → alternative use → dependence → priority** で考える

### 模範解答の読み方
一方へ多く配分すると他方が減るというopportunity costを示し、その後にcost・access・dependenceを判断基準としている

### 次へ
利用者数だけでなく、**realistic alternativeがあるか**を見る

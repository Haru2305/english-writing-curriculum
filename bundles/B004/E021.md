Reading
Urban water systems are designed to provide clean water every day, not only during emergencies. In normal conditions, efficiency matters. Cities try to reduce leakage, avoid unnecessary pumping, and operate treatment plants without maintaining large amounts of unused capacity.


A highly efficient system, however, can become vulnerable when demand rises suddenly or a drought reduces supply. If every treatment plant already operates close to its normal limit, there may be little room to respond when one facility fails. Reserve capacity looks expensive in ordinary years because part of it may remain unused.


This creates a familiar trade-off. Extra storage, backup wells, or connections to neighboring systems can increase cost, yet they may mitigate the impact of a disruption. A city that depends on one major source can operate efficiently for years and still face serious problems when that source becomes unavailable.


Allocation becomes especially important during shortages. Authorities may allocate more water to hospitals and other essential services while asking households to reduce non-essential use. Staff can be assigned to repair major leaks, and emergency water can be distributed to neighborhoods where the normal network has failed. Demand management can also reduce pressure on the system. Temporary restrictions on garden watering, industrial use, or car washing may preserve more water for essential purposes. However, restrictions need clear rules because the same percentage reduction can have very different effects on different users.


Fairness is not simple. An equal reduction for every user may seem neutral, but some users have very different needs. A hospital, a household, and a factory cannot always reduce consumption by the same percentage without different consequences. Infrastructure condition matters too. Older pipes can lose large amounts of treated water before it reaches users, so investing in repairs may sometimes save more water than asking households for additional cuts. Yet pipe replacement is expensive and can take years, which means cities must balance immediate restrictions with long-term investment.


Long-term planning therefore requires both efficiency and resilience. Cities should reduce avoidable waste, but they also need enough spare capacity to handle failures, droughts, and sudden demand. Building too much unused capacity can raise costs, while building too little can leave the system fragile. Planners may therefore compare several realistic scenarios rather than choose one fixed level of reserve. They may also test whether neighboring systems can share water during emergencies, because cooperation can sometimes provide resilience without requiring every city to build the same backup facilities independently. The most efficient system during ordinary weeks is not necessarily the system that protects residents best during a serious regional water crisis.

解説補強｜reserve capacityは「unused」ではなくinsurance

### この問題で考えること
通常時に使われないcapacityを単純なwasteとみなさず、**failure時にどの機能を残すか**で評価します。

### 考え方
operate near full capacity → normal-time efficiency
→ facility failure / drought時に spare room がない
→ service loss が大きくなる
reserve / alternative source → extra cost
→ disruption時のimpactをmitigate

### 弱い考え方との比較
× Unused capacity is inefficient, so it should be eliminated.
○ Some reserve capacity may be justified when the cost of losing essential supply is high.

### 次の問題に持っていくもの
system designでは **utilization / spare capacity / failure mode / consequence** をセットで見ます。病床、電力、交通にも転用できます。

Part 1｜Reading

Wearable devices can measure steps, heart rate, skin temperature, movement during sleep, and many other signals. Because these numbers are easy to collect, they can create the impression that health has become directly measurable. In reality, most devices measure indicators that are related to health rather than health itself.

A step count is a simple example. More movement may be associated with physical activity, but the same number of steps can represent very different levels of effort. Walking slowly on a flat surface and climbing stairs may produce similar counts while placing different demands on the body.

The same problem appears with more complex measurements. A device may estimate sleep by combining movement and heart-rate patterns. Yet lying still while awake can sometimes resemble sleep, and unusual movement during sleep can be misclassified as wakefulness. The device is not “seeing” sleep directly; it is interpreting signals through a model.

[ A ]

This distinction matters whenever a proxy is used for a hidden condition. A change in a measured signal may reflect the condition of interest, but it may also result from another cause. Higher skin temperature, for example, can be related to illness, room temperature, recent exercise, or how tightly the device touches the skin.

Researchers therefore compare wearable measurements with other forms of evidence. A laboratory measurement may be more precise under controlled conditions, while a wearable may capture patterns across ordinary daily life. Self-reports may provide information about pain or fatigue that no sensor can detect directly.

Combining methods does not guarantee agreement. In fact, disagreement can be informative. If a wearable reports poor sleep while a participant reports feeling rested, researchers must ask whether the device, the self-report, or the concept of “good sleep” is responsible for the mismatch.

Calibration also matters. A device tested on one group may perform differently on another group if skin properties, age, movement patterns, or medical conditions affect the signal. Even a technically precise sensor can produce a biased estimate if the model connecting the signal to the target outcome is poorly matched to the user.

For these reasons, uncertainty should be reported rather than hidden. A health estimate is more useful when users know what was measured directly, what was inferred, and how large the possible error may be.

The broader lesson is that convenient measurement does not eliminate interpretation. A number becomes meaningful only when its relationship to the underlying phenomenon is understood.

Model answer (98 words)

I think wearable devices can be useful for university health programs, but participation should be voluntary and the data should not be treated as a complete measure of health. A device can estimate steps or sleep, yet these numbers may be affected by how the device is worn or by conditions it cannot detect. For example, a student may record enough sleep time but still feel exhausted because of illness or stress. Universities should therefore use wearable data only as one source of information and should allow students to explain their own condition before any advice is given.

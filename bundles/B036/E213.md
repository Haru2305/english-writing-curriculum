Part 1｜Reading

Modern services often promise freedom by offering more choices. A streaming platform can display thousands of films, a university can offer many elective courses, and a mobile plan can be adjusted in dozens of ways. In one sense, more options clearly increase possibility. Yet possibility and usable choice are not the same thing.

To choose well, a person must notice differences, decide which differences matter, and predict how each option will fit future needs. When the number of options grows, the amount of comparison grows too. The difficulty becomes greater when options are described with different labels or when important features are distributed across several pages.

This burden is especially visible when people lack stable preferences. A student choosing a meal plan before arriving on campus may not know how often they will eat breakfast, how late classes will run, or whether friends will usually eat together. Asking the student to identify a single “best” plan may therefore demand a level of certainty that does not yet exist.

[ A ]

One response is to reduce the number of options. That can help, but reduction alone may remove a choice that matters to a smaller group. Another response is to organize options around meaningful criteria. Instead of showing ten plans as a flat list, a university might first ask whether the student values low cost, flexibility, or frequent meals, and then display the most relevant differences.

Defaults can also reduce effort. If most students choose a standard plan, making that plan the default may save time. However, a default is not neutral. People often remain with a preselected option even when changing it is easy. A poorly chosen default can therefore shift behavior rather than merely simplify a decision.

Decision aids work best when they make comparison easier without pretending to know the user’s priorities. A useful tool may show trade-offs, make uncommon fees visible, and allow the user to change the importance of different criteria. Its goal is not to make the decision disappear but to make the reasons for the decision clearer.

This distinction matters because satisfaction after a choice depends partly on expectations. A person who sees a recommendation as a guarantee may blame the system when circumstances change. A person who sees it as structured support is more likely to revise the decision when new information appears.

Good decision support therefore combines simplification with transparency. It reduces irrelevant complexity, preserves important differences, and leaves the final priority judgment to the person who will live with the result.

Model answer (107 words)
The university should first group the ten plans by meaningful criteria, such as cost, flexibility, and meal frequency, and show one typical option in each group. This reduces the number of comparisons students must make before they know which differences matter. A student who cares mainly about low cost could start with that group instead of comparing all ten plans. Students with less common needs should still be able to open a full list or filter by special conditions. The goal is not to remove choice, but to organize it so that uncertain first-year students can compare options without pretending that one plan is best for everyone.

Part 1｜Reading A

Recommendation systems are designed to help users find useful items in environments where the number of possible choices is too large to examine individually. A library platform may recommend books, a music service may suggest songs, and a university website may highlight courses that similar students selected.

Such systems often learn from previous behavior. If many users who borrowed one book also borrowed another, the second book may be recommended to future users with similar histories. This seems to reflect preference, but the relationship between recommendation and preference can become circular.

Once an item is recommended, it becomes more visible. Greater visibility increases the chance that users will click on it, borrow it, or rate it. Those actions then become new data showing that the item is popular. The system may therefore strengthen a pattern that it helped create.

[ A ]

This does not mean recommendations are inherently misleading. Increased visibility can be valuable when useful items would otherwise be difficult to discover. The problem is that observed popularity after recommendation cannot always be treated as a neutral measure of what users would have chosen without the recommendation.

Researchers can address this problem by comparing different exposure conditions. Some users may receive the usual recommendation while others see a more varied or partly random set. The goal is to estimate how much behavior reflects prior preference and how much is caused by what the system displayed.

Another issue is diversity. A system optimized only for immediate clicks may repeatedly recommend already popular items because they are safe choices. Users may receive fewer opportunities to encounter unfamiliar but relevant material.

Evaluation should therefore include more than the number of clicks or loans generated by recommendations. It should also consider whether users discover useful new options and whether the system narrows or broadens the range of items receiving attention.

Part 2｜Reading B

Institutions often use numerical indicators because numbers make comparison easier. A school can compare test scores, a hospital can track waiting times, and a workplace can measure how quickly tasks are completed. Indicators can reveal problems that would otherwise remain vague.

Difficulty begins when an indicator becomes not only a measure but also a target. If people know that their performance will be judged by one number, they may change behavior specifically to improve that number.

For example, if a support center is evaluated only by average call length, staff may learn to end conversations quickly. The measured value improves, but some users may need to call again because their problem was not resolved.

The issue is not that the indicator is false. Call length really does measure something. The problem is that it captures only one part of the broader goal, and optimization can shift effort toward the measured part while neglecting what remains unmeasured.

[ B ]

One response is to use several indicators. A support center might examine call length, repeat calls, user satisfaction, and whether the issue was resolved. No single measure becomes a complete definition of success.

However, adding more numbers is not enough by itself. Different indicators may conflict, and people still need to understand what each one represents. Quantitative measures should therefore be combined with direct observation, qualitative information, and explicit discussion of trade-offs.

A useful indicator is best treated as evidence rather than as the goal itself. It helps answer a question, but it should not silently replace the question.

Model answer (105 words)

I think universities should use several measures rather than a single score when evaluating whether students are learning effectively. A single number is easy to compare, but it may encourage teachers and students to focus only on what the test measures. For example, if a course is judged only by final-exam scores, students may memorize facts while receiving little practice in discussion or problem solving. Universities should combine exam results with assignments, practical tasks, and student explanations of their reasoning. This approach is less simple, but it gives a more balanced picture and reduces the risk of improving the metric instead of the learning itself.

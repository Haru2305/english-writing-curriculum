Passage

People who know a subject well are often expected to explain it well. Expertise certainly provides knowledge, examples, and a sense of which ideas matter most. Yet expertise can also create a communication problem. As people become skilled, many intermediate decisions that once required conscious effort become automatic.

Consider an experienced bicycle mechanic listening to a strange sound from a wheel. The mechanic may notice the rhythm, connect it with a particular part, and decide which component to inspect first. A beginner, however, may hear only “a noise.” If the mechanic says, “Check whether the wheel is true,” the instruction may be perfectly meaningful to another mechanic but incomplete for someone who does not know what signs to look for.

The difficulty is not that experts are unwilling to explain. Rather, they may no longer notice which steps have disappeared from conscious attention. A process that feels like one decision to an expert may contain several separate judgments for a novice.

This is why adding more information does not automatically solve the problem. A long explanation can still be unhelpful if it gives many facts without showing how those facts guide a decision. Novices often need structure: what to notice first, which differences matter, and what conclusion follows from each observation.

Examples can help, but examples also have limits. If every example is too similar, the learner may memorize surface features rather than understand the underlying rule. A medical student who sees only textbook-perfect cases, for instance, may struggle when a real patient shows only some of the expected signs.

One useful method is to ask the learner to explain the idea back in different words. This is not merely a test of memory. Rephrasing forces the learner to select relationships among ideas. Another method is transfer: asking the learner to apply the explanation to a new case. A person who can repeat an explanation but cannot use it may have learned the wording rather than the principle.

These checks also help the expert. If the learner gives an unexpected answer, the expert can identify where the explanation failed. The problem may be missing background knowledge, an ambiguous term, or an unstated intermediate step. In this sense, explanation is not a one-way delivery of information. It is a process of testing a shared model.

Successful explanation therefore cannot be judged by how fluent the speaker sounds. Experts often sound fluent precisely because their own processing is highly compressed. The relevant question is whether the listener can reconstruct enough of the hidden structure to reason independently.

This does not mean that every explanation should be maximally detailed. Too much detail can bury the main point and overload attention. The appropriate level depends on the listener’s goal. Someone who needs to perform a routine task may require a clear sequence, while someone who must solve unfamiliar problems needs to understand why the sequence works.

Adjusting an explanation to the listener is sometimes criticized as “oversimplifying.” But responsible adjustment does not require removing important truth. It means deciding which distinctions are necessary now, which can be postponed, and how understanding will be checked before greater complexity is added.

The broader lesson is that clarity is not a property of sentences alone. It is a relationship between an explanation, a learner’s prior knowledge, and the task the learner must eventually perform.

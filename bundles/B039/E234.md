Part 1｜Reading A

Clinical studies often measure outcomes that matter directly to patients: survival, pain, ability to function, or quality of life. These outcomes can take a long time to observe, especially when a disease progresses slowly.

Researchers therefore sometimes use surrogate endpoints. A surrogate is a measurement expected to predict a clinically important outcome. Blood pressure, a laboratory value, an imaging result, or the size of a tumor may be measured sooner than survival or long-term function.

Surrogates can accelerate research. If a treatment produces a large improvement in a marker that is strongly connected to later health, researchers may obtain useful evidence without waiting many years.

The danger is that changing the marker does not always change the outcome people actually care about. A treatment may improve a laboratory number through one biological pathway while causing harm through another pathway that the surrogate does not capture.

[ A ]

Validation is therefore essential. A good surrogate should not merely be associated with the clinical outcome. Changes in the surrogate caused by treatment should reliably correspond to changes in the patient-important outcome across relevant settings.

This requirement is stronger than correlation. Suppose patients with lower levels of a biomarker usually have better outcomes. A drug that lowers the biomarker may still fail to improve health if the biomarker is only a sign of disease rather than a cause or reliable mediator of treatment benefit.

Context also matters. A surrogate validated for one disease, treatment class, or population may not work equally well in another. The biological relationship can change when the mechanism of treatment changes.

Regulators and clinicians therefore consider both the strength of validation and the consequences of acting early. In a severe disease with few options, accepting uncertainty around a surrogate may be reasonable. When effective treatments already exist, stronger evidence may be required before replacing them.

Long-term follow-up remains important even after a treatment is approved using a surrogate. Later data can confirm whether the predicted clinical benefit actually appears and whether unexpected harms emerge.

The broader lesson is that a proxy can speed decision making only when users remember that it stands for something else. The convenience of an early measurement should never erase the distinction between the marker and the outcome it is meant to predict.

Part 2｜Reading B

Urban noise is usually summarized in decibels, but a single sound level does not fully describe how people experience noise. A brief loud event and a continuous moderate sound can have the same average level while producing different effects.

Timing matters. Noise during the day may interfere with conversation or concentration, while similar sound at night can disturb sleep. Predictability matters too. A steady sound may become easier to ignore than irregular bursts that repeatedly capture attention.

The meaning of a sound also changes perception. Music chosen by the listener may be experienced differently from construction noise at the same measured level. This does not make physical measurement irrelevant, but it shows that exposure and annoyance are not identical concepts.

Measurement location creates another challenge. A sensor on a major road may accurately describe traffic noise there but may not represent apartments behind buildings, upper floors, parks, or quieter side streets.

[ B ]

Researchers therefore combine several measures. They may record average sound level, peak events, nighttime exposure, duration, and the number of people affected. Surveys can add information about sleep disturbance, annoyance, or interference with daily activities.

However, self-reported annoyance is influenced by expectations and attitudes as well as acoustic exposure. Residents who distrust a noise source may report greater disturbance than residents who view the same source as necessary or beneficial.

Policy must therefore distinguish between measuring sound and deciding what level of burden is acceptable. A technically accurate map can identify exposure, but acceptable limits depend on health evidence, land use, feasibility, and social priorities.

Interventions also shift costs. A barrier may reduce noise for one neighborhood while reflecting sound toward another area. Rerouting traffic can improve one street and worsen another.

Good noise policy consequently asks not only how loud a city is on average, but when, where, and for whom harmful exposure occurs, and how proposed solutions redistribute that exposure.

## Final Gate｜Q3Bの読み方

この作文の核心は「community activity が良いか」ではなく、**全員必修という制度が教育目的を達成しつつ、学生ごとの制約に耐えられるか**。

### 考え方
**educational purpose → mechanism → distribution / constraint → safeguard → conditional requirement** の順で組む。

- Position：一定条件ならrequireしてよい
- Reason：教室外で academic knowledge と real problems を接続できる
- Mechanism：実際の相手・課題に触れることで、知識の使い方を具体化できる
- Support：health survey / tutoring / nonprofit data analysis
- Qualification：paid work・caregiving・disability・travel constraints は均等でない
- Safeguard：複数の達成方法＋reasonable exemptions
- Return：条件を満たせば educational requirement になる

### 弱い考え方との比較
× 「地域貢献は良い経験だから全員やるべき」  
○ 「教育効果のmechanismを示し、参加コストが偏る学生には代替手段を用意した上で必修化する」  

### 次の問題に持っていくもの
“Should all students be required to ...?” 型では、  
**目的は何か → その制度で本当に目的が達成されるか → 誰に負担が偏るか → 例外・代替で修正できるか**  
を60〜90秒で確認する。

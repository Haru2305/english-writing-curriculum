Part 1｜Reading A

Environmental restoration aims to improve ecosystems that have been damaged or altered. At first glance, success might seem easy to define: return the site to the condition it had before disturbance.

In practice, the phrase “before disturbance” is difficult. Ecosystems change over time even without human intervention. Climate varies, species migrate, fires and storms occur, and the historical record may be incomplete.

Restoration projects therefore need a reference condition rather than an imagined perfectly fixed past. The reference may come from nearby sites, historical records, old photographs, soil evidence, or knowledge of ecological processes.

Choosing a reference involves values as well as science. One community may prioritize native biodiversity, another flood protection, another recreation, and another cultural use of the landscape. These goals can overlap but may also conflict.

[ A ]

A second problem is that restoring structure does not guarantee restoring function. Planting the same tree species that once dominated an area may recreate the appearance of a forest while failing to restore soil processes, water flow, pollination, or animal communities.

Time scale matters too. Some ecological processes recover slowly. A project may show rapid increases in plant cover but require decades before mature habitat develops. Evaluation based only on the first few years can therefore favor interventions that produce visible short-term change.

Climate change adds another complication. Reconstructing a historical ecosystem exactly may be impossible or undesirable if future temperature and rainfall differ substantially from past conditions. Managers may instead aim for resilience: an ecosystem able to maintain important functions under changing conditions.

This does not mean history is irrelevant. Historical evidence helps identify what has been lost and which changes are recent. But restoration increasingly combines historical knowledge with forecasts about future conditions.

Success should therefore be judged against explicit goals. A project designed to reduce erosion should not be evaluated only by species counts, and a project designed to restore habitat should not be judged only by visual appearance.

Restoration is thus less like rebuilding a machine from a blueprint and more like guiding a dynamic system toward desired functions while recognizing uncertainty about both its past and future.

Part 2｜Reading B

People often learn from the behavior of others. A student may choose a course because friends recommend it, a consumer may try a restaurant because it is crowded, and a worker may adopt a practice after seeing colleagues use it.

This creates social information. Observing others can be efficient because each person does not need to investigate every option independently. When other people have useful experience, imitation can save time and reduce uncertainty.

However, popularity can also become self-reinforcing. An option that receives a small early advantage may attract more users, generating more reviews, more visibility, and more future users. The resulting popularity may partly reflect history rather than superior quality.

This makes causal interpretation difficult. If students who join a study group later receive higher grades, the group may have helped them. But students who choose to join may already be more motivated or better organized.

[ B ]

Random assignment can sometimes separate selection from influence, but it is not always practical or ethical. Researchers may instead use natural variation, timing differences, network structure, or repeated observations to estimate how much behavior spreads between people.

Social influence can also be heterogeneous. A recommendation from a close friend may matter more than one from a stranger. A behavior that is visible to others may spread differently from a private behavior.

Digital platforms amplify these processes because rankings, likes, and view counts make popularity visible. Showing popularity can help users find useful material quickly, but it can also magnify early random differences.

Designers therefore face a trade-off. Hiding all social information removes a potentially valuable signal, while displaying it prominently may make collective behavior less independent.

The key question is not simply whether people imitate others. It is whether observed similarity comes from influence, shared circumstances, or self-selection into similar groups.

Model answer (101 words)

I think real-time crowd information is useful because it can help students choose spaces efficiently instead of walking across campus only to find them full. It may also distribute demand more evenly by directing some students toward less crowded facilities. For example, a student who sees that the main library is nearly full could use a smaller study room nearby. However, the data should be updated frequently and should not reveal information about individual users. Universities should also show how the estimate is produced. With those safeguards, crowd information can save time and improve access without creating a significant privacy problem.

Part 1｜Reading A

Organizations often collect large amounts of data because storage has become inexpensive and analysis tools are widely available. More data can improve understanding, but collecting information also creates obligations.

The first obligation is relevance. Data should be connected to a clear purpose. If an organization collects information simply because it might become useful someday, it may accumulate sensitive details without knowing how they will be used.

The second obligation is quality. A large dataset can contain missing values, duplicate records, outdated categories, and measurements produced under different procedures. Quantity does not compensate automatically for inconsistency.

The third obligation is governance. People need to know who can access the data, how long the information will be retained, and under what conditions it can be shared. These decisions become more difficult after data have spread across many systems.

[ A ]

Data minimization is one response. The principle is to collect only information necessary for a defined task and to keep it only as long as needed. This can reduce privacy risk and make data easier to manage.

However, minimization creates trade-offs. Future researchers may discover a valuable use for information that was not retained. Historical datasets sometimes become important precisely because they contain variables that were not central to the original project.

The question is therefore not whether organizations should collect as little data as possible under every circumstance. It is whether the expected value of additional information justifies the cost of storing, protecting, documenting, and governing it.

Transparency can support this judgment. If organizations describe why information is collected and how long it will be kept, users can better understand the exchange involved.

A mature data strategy treats collection as a commitment rather than a free resource. Every new variable may create future analytical value, but it also creates responsibilities that remain after the original project ends.

Part 2｜Reading B

Public transport systems must balance frequency, capacity, and coverage. A bus that comes every five minutes is convenient, but running frequent service requires vehicles and drivers. A route that reaches every neighborhood may improve access but can become slow if it makes many deviations.

Frequency has a nonlinear effect on user experience. When buses are very frequent, passengers may arrive without checking a timetable. When service is infrequent, missing one vehicle can add a long delay.

Capacity interacts with frequency. A route may appear frequent on paper but still provide poor service if vehicles are repeatedly full and passengers cannot board.

Coverage adds another dimension. Direct routes between major destinations can carry many people efficiently, while local routes serving less populated areas may be essential for users who have no alternative.

[ B ]

Transport planners therefore distinguish network efficiency from individual accessibility. A redesign that increases average speed may still make travel worse for a group whose direct route disappears.

Transfers can improve network efficiency by allowing fewer routes to serve more origin-destination pairs. Yet transfers introduce waiting, uncertainty, and physical effort. Their burden is especially important for older passengers, people with disabilities, or travelers carrying children or luggage.

Reliability matters as much as scheduled frequency. Ten buses per hour do not provide a five-minute experience if several arrive together and then leave a long gap.

Evaluation should therefore combine operational measures with user outcomes: travel time, waiting, crowding, number of transfers, walking distance, and which populations experience gains or losses.

The best network is not simply the one with the highest average speed or the largest number of routes. It is the one whose design matches the goals the transport system is expected to serve.

Model answer (100 words)
Students should be allowed to add or drop courses without penalty during the first two weeks, but after that point changes should require a documented reason. This gives students enough time to discover whether a course matches their preparation and workload. At the same time, a firm deadline prevents students from holding scarce seats while other students remain unable to enroll. For example, a student could try two overlapping electives during week one but would need to release one seat before week three. The rule protects useful exploration while limiting the scheduling and teaching costs created by unlimited late changes.

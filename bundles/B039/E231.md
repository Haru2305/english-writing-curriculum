Part 1｜Reading A

People often assume that more information leads to better decisions. In many settings this is true. A patient benefits from knowing the major risks of treatment, a voter may benefit from understanding a policy, and a consumer may benefit from comparing prices and features.

But additional information is not always equally useful. Decision makers have limited attention and time. If essential facts are mixed with many low-value details, the important distinctions may become harder to notice.

This is especially relevant when information is presented in long lists. A hospital discharge document may contain dozens of instructions, warnings, contact numbers, and explanations. If the patient cannot identify which actions are urgent, technically complete information may fail in practice.

Prioritization can help. Information can be organized by action: what must be done today, what should be monitored, and what can be reviewed later. This does not necessarily remove detail; it changes the order in which detail is encountered.

[ A ]

Personalization can also improve relevance. A general guide may mention risks that do not apply to a particular person. Removing irrelevant information can make the remaining material easier to use. Yet excessive personalization has risks if important possibilities are hidden because a system incorrectly judges them irrelevant.

Visual design matters as well. Headings, spacing, tables, and warnings can direct attention, but emphasis should correspond to actual importance. If every sentence is highlighted, highlighting loses its function.

Researchers evaluating information materials therefore ask not only how much knowledge users gain but whether they can perform the intended task: take the correct medicine, recognize an emergency sign, or choose between options.

The goal is not minimum information. It is usable information. A successful design preserves necessary detail while helping users identify what matters for the decision they must make.

Part 2｜Reading B

Researchers studying human behavior often ask participants to report how frequently they performed an activity in the past: how much they exercised, what they ate, how often they felt stressed, or how many hours they slept.

Self-report is practical, but memory is reconstructive. People do not store a complete record of every event and then retrieve it perfectly. They estimate from typical routines, memorable episodes, and beliefs about themselves.

The length of the recall period affects accuracy. Remembering what happened yesterday may be relatively easy. Estimating an average across several months requires more reconstruction.

Question wording also matters. Asking “How many times did you exercise last week?” may produce a different response from “On how many days did you exercise for at least 30 minutes?” even when both questions are intended to measure similar behavior.

[ B ]

Social expectations can introduce another source of error. Participants may underreport behaviors viewed negatively or overreport behaviors viewed positively. Anonymity can reduce this pressure but does not eliminate memory problems.

Objective measures can help, but they also have limitations. A wearable device may record movement accurately while failing to capture the purpose of the activity. A food receipt shows purchases but not who consumed the food or how much was eaten.

Researchers therefore often combine methods. Repeated short surveys can reduce recall burden, while devices or records can provide another source of evidence. Disagreement between methods is not automatically a problem; it can reveal what each method actually measures.

The strongest interpretation asks whether the measurement process is likely to create systematic error and whether that error could explain the observed result.

Model answer (105 words)
The reflection should ask two questions: What caused your most important error, and what will you change before the next assessment? Students should receive credit for giving a specific explanation and a realistic next action, not for claiming that they worked hard or felt disappointed. For example, a student who lost points through rushed reading might plan to reserve five minutes for checking question wording next time. The task should be brief and worth only a small amount of credit. Grading specificity rather than self-praise would make the reflection useful for future improvement and reduce the incentive to write empty comments that simply sound responsible.

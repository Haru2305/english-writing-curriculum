Part 1｜Reading A

Early-warning systems are designed to detect danger before the worst consequences occur. They may alert communities to floods, hospitals to clinical deterioration, computer networks to attacks, or factories to equipment failure. Their value seems obvious: earlier information can create time for action.

The difficulty is that warnings are decisions under uncertainty. A system must choose a threshold. If the threshold is very sensitive, it may detect more genuine threats but also produce more false alarms. If the threshold is strict, false alarms may fall while some real threats are missed.

The cost of a false alarm is not always small. Evacuations interrupt work, transport, and daily life. Medical alerts demand attention from staff who are already busy. Security warnings can stop ordinary activity. When false alarms occur frequently, users may begin to ignore them.

This creates a feedback problem. A technically accurate alert is useful only if people notice it, understand it, and respond appropriately. If prior warnings have often proved unimportant, trust can decline even when the next warning is serious.

[ A ]

Designers therefore need to evaluate more than sensitivity. They should ask which errors are most costly, how often users can realistically respond, and whether the warning explains what action is recommended.

Context also matters. A flood warning for a densely populated valley may justify a lower threshold than an alert for a minor inconvenience. The same numerical probability can support different actions when the consequences differ.

Warning systems can also separate levels of urgency. Instead of issuing only “safe” or “danger,” they may use watch, warning, and emergency categories. This allows people to prepare before the strongest action is required.

However, adding categories can create confusion if the distinctions are unclear. A warning system succeeds when its categories map onto practical decisions that users can understand.

The broader lesson is that early warning is not only a prediction problem. It is a communication and decision-design problem in which accuracy, false alarms, missed events, user trust, and consequences must be considered together.

Part 2｜Reading B

Cities often plant trees to reduce heat. Tree canopies provide shade and can also cool the surrounding air through evaporation from leaves. This has led many cities to use tree-planting programs as part of climate adaptation.

Measuring the effect is more complicated than comparing neighborhoods with many trees to neighborhoods with few trees. Tree-rich areas may differ in building density, road width, income, elevation, proximity to water, or other features that also affect temperature.

The type and maturity of trees matter too. A recently planted sapling provides much less shade than a mature tree. Different species have different canopy shapes, water needs, and seasonal patterns.

Researchers therefore need measurements that match the question. Satellite images can estimate surface temperature across a wide area, but surface temperature is not identical to the air temperature experienced by a pedestrian. Ground sensors measure local air conditions but cover fewer locations.

[ B ]

Time is another issue. A tree may reduce afternoon heat strongly but have little effect at night. Seasonal differences can also change the value of shade. A study using one summer week may therefore estimate a different effect from a study covering multiple years.

Equity questions further complicate evaluation. A citywide increase in tree cover may look successful while neighborhoods with the greatest heat exposure receive little improvement. Average canopy percentage can hide unequal distribution.

For policy, researchers may therefore combine several outcomes: local temperature, pedestrian shade, access to green space, maintenance cost, water use, and the distribution of benefits across neighborhoods.

Tree planting can be useful even when its average temperature effect is modest. It may improve comfort, reduce building energy use, support biodiversity, or provide public space. But these benefits should be distinguished rather than compressed into one number.

A strong evaluation asks not only whether trees are associated with cooler places, but which trees, in which locations, measured in what way, and benefiting whom.

Model answer (105 words)
Students should see response rates, summary ratings for several dimensions, and recurring themes from written comments, but not raw comments that could identify individuals or give one extreme opinion too much weight. The page should also state that course evaluations measure student perceptions, not teaching quality directly. For example, a course with a low workload rating might still require difficult independent study. Showing only one average score could hide that difference. Summarizing several indicators and reporting how many students responded would make the evidence easier to interpret while reducing the chance that a small or unusual group is treated as representative of the whole class.

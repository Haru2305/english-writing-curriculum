Part 1｜Reading A

Public policies are often evaluated by comparing what happened before and after a new rule was introduced. If an outcome improves after the policy, it is tempting to attribute the change to the policy itself.

The problem is that many other things can change at the same time. Economic conditions may improve, public awareness may rise, new technology may appear, or the population may change. A before-and-after difference therefore mixes the effect of the policy with other changes occurring over the same period.

A comparison group can help. If one region introduces a policy while a similar region does not, researchers can compare changes in both places. The relevant question becomes not simply whether the treated region improved, but whether it improved more than the comparison region.

This approach still depends on assumptions. The two regions need not be identical, but their trends before the policy should provide evidence that they were moving in comparable ways. If one region was already improving faster, the later difference may not be caused by the policy.

[ A ]

Timing can also create misleading conclusions. People may change behavior before a formal rule begins because they know it is coming. Alternatively, implementation may take months, so an effect may not appear immediately.

Researchers therefore often examine a range of time periods around the policy change. A sudden shift exactly when implementation begins can support a causal interpretation, while a change that starts much earlier may point to another explanation.

Policy effects can also vary across groups. A rule may improve the average outcome while helping one group and harming another. Reporting only the overall mean can therefore hide important distributional effects.

Finally, compliance matters. A policy written into law may be weakly enforced or followed differently across regions. Evaluating the formal rule without measuring actual exposure can exaggerate or underestimate its practical effect.

Good policy evaluation thus asks what changed, compared with what, when the change occurred, who was affected, and how fully the policy was implemented.

Part 2｜Reading B

Digital archives make enormous collections available to researchers and the public. Search tools allow users to find names, phrases, dates, or topics across thousands of documents in seconds.

This convenience changes research behavior. When people worked mainly with physical archives, they often browsed folders and encountered documents they had not specifically requested. Digital search encourages a more targeted process: enter a term, examine the results, and refine the query.

Targeted search is efficient, but it can make the archive appear to contain only what the search engine retrieves. A document may be relevant even when it does not contain the expected vocabulary. Historical actors may have used different terminology, spelling may vary, and scanned text may contain recognition errors.

Search ranking creates another layer of selection. Results displayed first receive more attention. The ranking may depend on exact word matches, document popularity, metadata quality, or technical choices that users never see.

[ B ]

Metadata can both help and limit discovery. Dates, locations, authors, and subject labels allow useful filtering, but labels are created by people and may reflect the categories available when the archive was organized.

Researchers can reduce these problems by varying search terms, examining neighboring documents, reading collection descriptions, and checking how digitization and indexing were performed. They may also compare digital results with catalog records or physical materials that were not scanned.

The goal is not to reject digital search. Search makes collections accessible at a scale that was previously impossible. The important point is to treat search results as a route into an archive, not as a complete map of everything the archive contains.

Model answer (102 words)
Students could choose between two preset weighting plans: 60 percent exams and 40 percent assignments, or 40 percent exams and 60 percent assignments. Everyone would still complete both forms of assessment, and the choice would have to be made before the first major score is returned. These rules preserve comparability because every student is judged on the same two skills within a limited range. Complete freedom would let students avoid whichever assessment exposes a weakness and would make final grades mean different things across students. A constrained choice provides flexibility without turning the grading system into a different course for each person.

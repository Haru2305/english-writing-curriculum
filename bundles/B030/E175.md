E175｜可視事例の偏り：見えている例だけで判断しない

P3 / LT03 / A25 / RQ04 / RQ09 / RQ13 / D04 / TF12

今日の狙い
初見概念「survivorship bias」を本文と表から構築し、現在観察できる事例だけを元の集団全体の特徴として扱わない。

時間配分
Silent Plan 1分 / Reading 18分 / Table 7分 / Questions 13分 / Claim Check 8分 / Short Output 8分 / Self-check 2分 / Post-solve 3分 ＝ Core 60分

Reading
[1] A digital-product team wants to learn which design habits make study apps remain useful over several years. Staff examine apps that are still listed in an online store three years after release and notice several common features: frequent updates, clear help pages, and simple navigation.

[2] These observations may be useful, but the sample has a hidden limitation. The team begins with apps that are still easy to find. Products that were removed from the store or abandoned by their developers are much less likely to appear in the analysis.

[3] This can produce survivorship bias. The bias occurs when analysis focuses on cases that remain observable after a selection process while overlooking cases that disappeared from observation. The visible group may have unusual features precisely because it remained visible.

[4] The problem is not that long-lasting apps teach us nothing. They may reveal useful practices. The problem is that without information about removed apps, we cannot tell whether those practices were unique to the apps that remained listed. A removed app may also have received frequent updates or used simple navigation.

[5] The team later reconstructs part of the original group from archived release records.

Table
Status after 3 years | Apps | Frequent updates | Simple navigation
Still listed | 120 | 91 | 84
Removed from store | 80 | 55 | 52
Status unclear | 42 | — | —

[6] The table changes the interpretation. Frequent updates are more common among the apps still listed, but they also appear in many removed apps. Simple navigation shows a similar pattern. Neither feature is therefore a guarantee that an app will remain available.

[7] A second uncertainty remains: the 42 apps with unclear status may differ from both known groups. Recovering older records reduces the sampling problem but does not remove all uncertainty.

[8] The broader lesson is that evidence can be distorted by the process that determines which cases remain visible. Before drawing conclusions from successful examples, we should ask which missing cases are absent from view.

Questions
Q1｜RQ04
Define survivorship bias from the passage.

Q2｜RQ13
How does the table weaken a simple claim that frequent updates determine long-term availability?

Q3｜RQ09
What can reasonably be inferred about simple navigation?

Q4｜D04
Explain how selecting only visible apps can create a misleading causal story.

Claim Check
Judge and repair:
1. Since many long-lasting apps received frequent updates, frequent updates guarantee long-term availability.
2. Studying visible examples can still be useful if missing cases are considered.
3. Adding information about removed apps eliminates all uncertainty.

Short Output
In 65–85 English words, explain survivorship bias and why the original analysis could mislead the team.

Self-check
□ I asked which cases were missing.
□ I used both listed and removed-app rows.
□ I did not turn association into proof of causation.
□ I kept the unclear-status group as remaining uncertainty.

解答・解説・自己修正

Q1｜解答例
Survivorship bias occurs when analysis emphasizes cases that remain observable while cases that disappeared are overlooked, causing the visible group to give a distorted picture of the original population.

Q2｜解答例
Many removed apps also received frequent updates, so the feature is not unique to the still-listed group and cannot by itself explain long-term availability.

Q3｜解答例
Simple navigation is more common among apps still listed, but it also appears in many removed apps, so it may be associated with long-term availability without guaranteeing it.

Q4｜解答例
The team selects apps that remain visible, observes features common among them, and then treats those features as explanations without checking whether the missing group had the same features.

Claim Check
1. Too strong → Frequent updates may be associated with long-term availability, but the table does not show that they guarantee it.
2. Supported.
3. Too strong → The additional records reduce the bias, but the unclear-status group still creates uncertainty.

Model output
Survivorship bias occurs when conclusions are based mainly on cases that remain visible while disappeared cases are overlooked. The team’s original analysis could mislead because it examined only apps still listed after three years. Features common among those apps might also have been common among apps later removed. Recovering the original release group helps test this possibility, although apps with unclear status still leave uncertainty.

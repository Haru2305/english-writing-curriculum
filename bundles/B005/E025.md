Reading
When a possible health risk is first detected, officials rarely know everything at once. They may know that several people have similar symptoms or that a common exposure is being investigated, while the exact cause remains uncertain. Communicating this early stage is difficult because the public wants clear answers before the evidence is complete.


One poor strategy is to hide uncertainty until every detail is confirmed. This can make later changes in advice look like contradictions. Another poor strategy is to communicate every possibility without explaining which ones are supported by evidence. That approach can create unnecessary fear.


A useful message separates what is known from what is still being investigated. Officials can communicate confirmed facts, such as where cases have been identified, while stating clearly that the source has not yet been established. They can also convey how serious the situation appears at that moment without claiming that the worst outcome is certain.


Action should be separated from prediction. People may not need to know the final cause before taking a simple precaution. For example, advice to avoid a specific exposure, monitor symptoms, or seek care under certain conditions can be reasonable even while the investigation continues. Clear action guidance helps people respond to uncertainty rather than wait passively for perfect information.


Messages also need to change when evidence changes. An update should explain what new information was found and why the recommendation has changed. If officials simply announce a new rule without connecting it to the evidence, people may interpret the change as inconsistency rather than learning. Communication channels matter too. A website may reach people who actively search for information, while text messages, local clinics, schools, or community organizations may reach people who would otherwise miss an update. Repeating the same core message across several channels can reduce confusion, provided that the wording remains consistent.


Trust does not require certainty. It requires that the strength of the message match the strength of the evidence. Officials can express concern, communicate confirmed information, and convey urgency when action is needed, while still acknowledging what remains unknown. Different audiences may also need different levels of detail, but the core distinction between confirmed facts, remaining uncertainty, and recommended action should stay consistent.

### この問題で考えること
「確定していない」と「何も行動できない」を同一視しません。**evidenceの確かさと、今取るactionの必要性を別に判断**します。

### 考え方
confirmed facts → 現在言える範囲
uncertain cause / future course → claim strengthを下げる
low-cost / high-benefit precaution → uncertaintyが残っていても推奨できる場合がある

### 弱い考え方との比較
× Officials should wait until everything is certain.
○ Officials can communicate confirmed facts, mark what remains uncertain, and recommend proportionate precautions when delay could matter.

### 次の問題に持っていくもの
risk communicationでは **certainty of evidence ≠ urgency of action**。両者を別々に評価します。

Reading
Cities often face difficult decisions about old buildings. A historic structure may have architectural or cultural value, yet maintaining it can be expensive. If the building is no longer used for its original purpose, officials may be asked whether public money should continue to support it.


One option is complete preservation. This can protect the building's physical form and historical character, but it may require large maintenance costs even when few people use the site. Another option is demolition and new construction, which may create space for housing, offices, or public facilities but permanently removes the original structure.


Adaptive reuse creates a middle path. A former station might become a library, a warehouse might become a market, or an old school might become community space. The building changes function while important features are preserved. Such projects can generate income or public use that helps support maintenance.


However, reuse also involves trade-offs. Modern safety standards may require elevators, emergency exits, reinforcement, or new utilities. These changes can affect the original appearance. A project that preserves every historical detail may be difficult to use, while a project that changes too much may retain little of the heritage value that justified preservation.


Public funding adds another question. A city cannot invest in every historic building equally. It may allocate more resources to structures with unusual significance, high public use, or few alternatives. Transparent criteria are important because preservation decisions can benefit some neighborhoods more than others. Community preferences can differ as well. Some residents may value the building mainly as a physical record of local history, while others may care more about keeping the site active and accessible. Reversible changes can sometimes reduce this conflict: a new interior element may be designed so that it can later be removed without destroying important original material. Consultation does not guarantee agreement, but it can clarify which features people consider essential and which changes they are willing to accept.


The best solution therefore depends on what exactly should be preserved and what changes are acceptable. Preservation does not always require keeping every feature unchanged. In some cases, adapting a building can keep its history visible while allowing it to serve a useful role in the present.

モデル答案
Preserving a historic building does not always mean keeping every feature unchanged. A city may invest in safety improvements while protecting the parts that carry the greatest historical value. Adaptive reuse can keep the building visible and useful instead of treating preservation and change as complete opposites.

解説補強｜二択の間にdesign optionを作る

### この問題で考えること
preserve / demolish の二択ではなく、**何を価値の核として残し、何を変更可能とするか**を分解します。

### 考え方
heritage valueを構成するfeaturesを特定
→ safety / accessibility / current use のrequirementsと衝突点を確認
→ important featuresをpreserveしつつ selected changes をadapt
→ public useやmaintenanceとのtrade-offを調整

### 弱い考え方との比較
× Preservation means changing nothing.
○ Preservation can protect the features that carry historical value while allowing reversible or necessary changes for continued use.

### 次の問題に持っていくもの
二者択一に見える問題では、**goalを構成要素へ分け、中間designを作れないか**を考えます。

Reading
[1] Cities often evaluate public events by counting visitors. Attendance is useful because it shows whether people came, but it does not reveal why they came, what they gained, or who could not participate. A crowded event can still fail to reach the population it was intended to serve.
[2] Organizers can therefore combine attendance with other evidence. Short surveys may show whether participants discovered a new service, met people outside their usual group, or changed their view of a local issue. Repeat attendance can indicate continuing interest, while geographic or age patterns can reveal whether participation is concentrated in a narrow part of the community.
[3] These measures also have limits. Surveys often attract people who are already willing to respond, and a positive answer immediately after an event does not prove a lasting effect. Organizers should therefore avoid turning every favorable response into a claim about long-term social change.
[4] Evaluation becomes more useful when it is linked to the original purpose of the event. If the goal was to introduce a public service, awareness may matter more than total attendance. If the goal was to connect different groups, the diversity of participation may matter more than how many people passed through the venue.
[5] The broader lesson is that success indicators should follow the question being asked. No single measure is universally best. A good evaluation uses several kinds of evidence, recognizes their limits, and selects them according to the intended outcome.
[6] The choice of evidence can also change behavior. If organizers know that success will be judged only by attendance, they may design an event to maximize numbers even when the original purpose was deeper engagement with a smaller group. Evaluation measures are therefore not neutral after-the-fact descriptions; they can influence planning before the event occurs. This is another reason to connect indicators clearly to the intended outcome rather than using whatever number is easiest to collect.

RQ11｜要約

main claim + necessary support + limitation を自分の文構造で再構成する。

Summary Task｜解答例
Attendance is useful for evaluating public events, but it cannot show by itself who benefited or whether the event met its goal. Organizers should combine several measures and choose them according to the intended outcome, such as awareness or diverse participation. They should also recognize that surveys and immediate reactions do not necessarily demonstrate lasting social effects.

Inference Guide
推論は「本文にないことを自由に考える」ことではない。
explicit fact A + explicit fact B → limited conclusion
結論の強さは、根拠より強くしない。
Reading
[1] Historical maps often contain empty areas that modern readers may interpret as places the mapmaker did not know. Sometimes that is correct. In other cases, however, the blank area reflects a decision about what the map was for. A map designed to show tax districts may omit mountains that mattered greatly to travelers, while a route map may ignore administrative boundaries.
[2] The same place can therefore appear very differently across maps produced in the same period. One map may name villages but not rivers; another may show rivers clearly while reducing settlements to small marks. These differences are not necessarily errors. They can reveal which relationships the maker considered useful for the intended audience.
[3] Marginal notes provide another clue. A short comment such as “road difficult in winter” may appear beside a route even when weather is not represented elsewhere on the map. The note suggests that the map was used in situations where seasonal travel mattered. It does not prove exactly who carried the map or how often the route was used.
[4] Historians therefore compare maps with other records and with one another. Repeated omissions can be informative if they follow the same purpose, while a single unexplained blank should be treated cautiously. The absence of a feature is evidence only when the reader can identify what kind of representation the map was trying to provide.
[5] This approach changes the question from “Why did the mapmaker fail to include everything?” to “Which information was selected, and for what use?” The difference encourages readers to interpret omission as a possible design choice before treating it as ignorance.
[6] Inference becomes more difficult when several explanations remain possible. If a map repeatedly omits minor roads, the omission may reflect the map’s purpose, the limited space available, or the fact that those roads were unimportant to the intended audience. The safest conclusion is often not to choose one explanation immediately, but to identify which possibilities are supported and what additional evidence would be needed to distinguish among them. Good inference narrows uncertainty without pretending to eliminate it.

Reading answers
Q1｜解答例
It can reflect the map’s purpose and a choice about which information was useful for the intended audience.
別解：紙面の制約（限られたスペース）や、想定読者にとって重要でない情報だったことも可（[6]）。
Q2｜B
本文は“suggests”と限定し、seasonal travel matteredまでは言えるが、who used it / frequencyまではproofではない。
Q3｜解答例
The maps may have been designed for different purposes or audiences, so the different omissions may reflect different priorities rather than simple errors.
別解：どちらかが誤りとは限らず、目的・紙面の制約・想定読者にとっての重要度など複数の説明が残るので、追加の証拠なしには一つに決められない（[6]）。
Q4｜解答例
Because an omission is informative only when the reader can connect it to the type of representation or purpose of the map.
A07｜推論
推論距離を管理する。
本文：winter difficulty note exists
＋本文：note suggests seasonal travel mattered
→ 推論：seasonal conditions were relevant
× merchants certainly used it
× route was always dangerous

Passage
A fictional water network uses a “reserve margin” when planning daily supply. The reserve margin is the difference between the amount of water the system expects to have available and the amount it expects users to need during a high-demand period.
The margin is not wasted water. It provides room for forecast error, temporary equipment problems, or demand that is slightly higher than expected. [A] A system with no reserve may appear highly efficient on an ordinary day but become fragile when several small problems occur together.
A very large reserve also has costs. Maintaining unused pumping or storage capacity can require money, land, and energy. The goal is therefore not to maximize the margin but to choose enough flexibility to handle plausible variation without creating excessive cost.
Forecast quality changes the size of the useful reserve. If demand can be predicted accurately and backup equipment is reliable, the network may need less spare capacity. If forecasts are uncertain or repairs take a long time, a larger reserve can have more value.
Candidate Sentence
In this sense, the margin converts some unused capacity into protection against uncertainty.
FAQ
Q: Does a larger reserve always mean better service?
A: No. Beyond a useful level, extra capacity may add cost without much additional protection.
Q: Is the reserve based only on average demand?
A: No. Planners pay particular attention to high-demand periods and uncertainty around forecasts.
Q: Can conservation reduce the needed reserve?
A: It may, if it reliably lowers or stabilizes peak demand.

解答・解説・自己修正
Q1｜解答例
The reserve margin is the extra supply capacity available above expected high-period demand, kept to absorb uncertainty, forecast error, and temporary system problems.
Q2｜解答
A. The previous sentence lists uncertainties the reserve can absorb, and the Candidate Sentence summarizes their function before the passage gives the consequence of having no reserve.
Q3｜解答例
When forecasts are less reliable, actual demand is more likely to exceed the expected level. Additional reserve capacity provides room for that prediction error and reduces the risk that small problems combine into a shortage.
Q4｜解答例
The table assigns a larger reserve when forecast error, demand variability, and backup weakness are greater, showing that the appropriate margin depends on system conditions rather than one fixed percentage.
Q5｜解答例
Scenario A may operate safely with a smaller reserve because uncertainty is low and backup reliability is high, though the table does not prove that zero reserve would be sufficient.
Model summary
A reserve margin is spare water-supply capacity kept above expected peak demand to protect against forecast error and temporary problems. It improves resilience but is not free, because excess capacity requires resources to maintain. The appropriate margin therefore depends on conditions such as forecast accuracy, demand variability, and backup reliability. Systems with greater uncertainty may justify a larger reserve, while more predictable systems may need less.
Model writing
Planners should judge the reserve margin by whether it prevents shortages during realistic high-demand and equipment-failure scenarios without imposing excessive capacity costs. They should compare actual peak demand, forecast error, and backup performance with the amount of spare capacity maintained. A margin that is never used is not automatically wasteful if it protects against plausible disruption, but very large unused capacity may be inefficient. The current table only gives qualitative scenarios, so planners would still need long-term operating data and cost estimates before selecting a specific target. That comparison would make resilience costs more transparent.

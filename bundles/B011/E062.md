Strategy

Reading
[1] International aid programs are often judged by highly visible outcomes: the number of schools built, clinics opened, or wells installed. These figures are useful, but they can create a misleading impression if they are treated as complete measures of success. A new facility may expand access, yet its long-term value also depends on staffing, maintenance, local trust, and whether people can actually use the service.


[2] This is why some evaluations distinguish outputs from outcomes. An output is something a project directly produces, such as a training course or a new water system. An outcome is a change in people’s lives that may follow, such as improved attendance or more reliable access to clean water. A project can therefore meet its output target without achieving the broader outcome that originally justified it.


[3] Even outcome measures need caution. If school attendance rises after a transport program begins, the program may have contributed to the change. However, the increase could also reflect lower fees, demographic change, or another policy introduced at the same time. An evaluation can strengthen a causal claim by comparing groups or time periods carefully, but it may still be unable to remove every alternative explanation.


[4] None of this means that aid cannot be evaluated. It means that confidence should match the design of the evidence. Directly observed outputs can often be described with relatively high certainty. Claims about social outcomes require more context, and claims about causes require stronger comparisons. The best reports make these differences visible rather than presenting every positive change as proof of project success.


[5] This distinction also affects how programs should be compared. A project with modest visible outputs may still produce meaningful outcomes if local institutions can maintain it, while a project with impressive construction figures may have limited effect if services cannot continue. Evaluators therefore need to ask not only how much was delivered but also whether the change lasted, who was reached, and what would probably have happened without the program. These questions make evaluation slower, but they reduce the risk of treating activity itself as evidence of impact.

Reading answers
Q1｜D
Paragraph 3はoutcome測定を否定せず、因果の別説明を加えて主張の射程を限定している。


Q2｜C
“may have contributed” “could also reflect” “may still be unable”が並び、因果への支持はあるが断定していない。


Q3｜A
may have contributed が最も直接に確信度を弱めている。


Q4｜解答例
Different study designs support different levels of certainty. Directly observed outputs can be described more confidently than broader outcomes or causal effects.


Evidence Output｜例
Paragraph 3 qualifies the previous discussion because it adds alternative explanations (lower fees, demographic change, another policy) for the rise in attendance. The phrase “may have contributed” shows that the author does not make a fully certain causal claim.


RQ10｜態度・確信度問題
内容問題と違い、「筆者が賛成か反対か」だけでは足りない。
certain / likely / possible / uncertain のどこに置くかを、本文のhedging・評価語・助動詞で根拠づける。

Reading
[1] Ecological restoration is often described as the effort to return a damaged environment to an earlier state. That description is useful, but it can hide a difficult question: earlier than what? Rivers change course, forests change species composition, and coastlines move even without direct human intervention. A restoration target therefore cannot be chosen simply by finding an old photograph and reproducing what it shows.


[2] A more practical approach begins with functions rather than appearances. The long-term ability of a wetland to store water during heavy rain, provide breeding areas for multiple species, and recover after periods of drought may matter more than whether the site resembles a particular historical moment. These functions, which can sometimes be supported by different combinations of species and landscape features, give planners a broader set of options.


[3] This flexibility does not remove the need for historical knowledge. Records of past vegetation, water flow, land use, and local species can reveal how an ecosystem changed and which changes were reversible. They can also identify losses that would otherwise be treated as natural. The difficult task is to use such records as evidence without assuming that the past offers a single correct design for the future.


[4] The choice becomes more complex when climate conditions are shifting. A species mix that survived well several decades ago may be poorly suited to future heat or rainfall patterns. Restoring a historical arrangement exactly, although attractive as a clear goal, could therefore reduce resilience rather than increase it. For this reason, some restoration plans combine historical evidence with projections of future conditions and with repeated monitoring after intervention.


[5] The central issue is not whether history or future adaptation should win. It is how evidence from both can be combined to define a target that is ecologically meaningful, realistically maintainable, and open to revision as conditions change.


[6] Monitoring is important for another reason. Restoration itself changes the environment, and the response may differ from what planners predicted. Measurements collected after intervention can reveal whether water storage, species diversity, or recovery from disturbance is improving. They can also show when an apparently successful change creates a new problem elsewhere. A restoration plan that includes repeated measurement and predefined points for revision is therefore not a sign of uncertainty in the weak sense; it is a way of treating uncertainty as part of the design.

解答・解説・自己修正


Structure Check
1. head noun = ability
2. store / provide / recover are parallel infinitive complements describing the wetland’s ability
3. Restoring a historical arrangement exactly could therefore reduce resilience rather than increase it.


Q1｜解答例
特定の過去の景観に似せることより、水を蓄え、生物を支え、干ばつ後に回復する生態系の機能。
採点単位：appearanceとの対比 / 複数のecological functions。


Q2｜解答例
過去の状態を正確に復元することは明確な目標として魅力的ではあるが、そのために回復力を高めるどころか、かえって低下させる可能性がある。


Q3｜C
history / function / changing conditions の3軸を統合した選択肢だけが全文を説明する。


Q4｜解答例
Because future heat or rainfall patterns may differ from past conditions, species or arrangements that once worked well may no longer be resilient.


Q5｜A
“may be poorly suited” signals possibility rather than certainty, so it directly marks the writer’s confidence level.


Q6｜解答例
Paragraph 6 first establishes monitoring as the topic. The next sentence keeps that topic active through “Measurements collected after intervention” and then adds what those measurements can reveal, creating an old→new information flow.

Reading
A new treatment can look promising before all of its benefits and risks are known. Early studies may show improvement in symptoms or laboratory measures, but the number of participants may be small and the follow-up period may be short. These limitations affect what can reasonably be concluded.


Researchers should acknowledge uncertainty without ignoring encouraging evidence. If a treatment helped many participants in an early trial, that result may justify further study. It does not automatically prove that the treatment is better for every patient or safe over many years. The type of outcome matters too. Improvement in a laboratory marker may be encouraging, but patients may care more about symptoms, daily function, hospitalization, or survival. A treatment can change a surrogate measure without producing the same size of benefit in outcomes that matter directly to patients.


Rare adverse effects create a particular challenge. A small study may be large enough to detect a common benefit but too small to reveal an uncommon harm. Longer follow-up may also identify effects that were not visible during the original trial. Decision makers therefore need to recognize the importance of both probability and severity.


Patients may differ in how they weigh these factors. A person with a severe condition and few alternatives may accept more uncertainty in exchange for a possible benefit. Another person with a mild condition and several established options may prefer stronger evidence before choosing the new treatment. The comparison treatment also matters. A new therapy that performs slightly better than no treatment is not necessarily better than the current standard of care. Decision makers need to ask what the intervention was compared with, how large the difference was, and whether the benefit was clinically meaningful rather than merely measurable.


Transparency matters when evidence changes. If later data reveal a previously unknown problem, researchers should admit errors if earlier analyses were incorrect and acknowledge new limitations if the evidence remains uncertain. Updating a conclusion is not the same as pretending the original evidence never existed. Monitoring after wider use can be important because rare harms may become visible only when many more people receive the treatment. A cautious decision can therefore include clear, revisable conditions: use may be expanded gradually, outcomes may be tracked carefully over time, and recommendations may be revised if the balance of benefit and harm changes.


A promising intervention should therefore be judged carefully by expected benefit, the magnitude and probability of harm, and the remaining uncertainty around both. Acknowledging uncertainty can support better decisions because it prevents possible benefit from being confused with guaranteed benefit.

### この問題で考えること
「promisingだから使う」「uncertainだから使わない」の二択ではなく、**expected benefitとharmのmagnitude・probability・uncertaintyを同時に評価**します。

### 考え方
early benefit signal
→ possible benefit
一方 small sample / short follow-up
→ rare or delayed harmが見えていない可能性
→ condition severity / alternativesによってacceptable uncertaintyが変わる
→ monitoring付きのconditional decision

### 弱い考え方との比較
× The treatment is promising, so it is safe.
○ A promising treatment may justify further use or study while long-term harms and uncertainty are still monitored.

### 模範解答の読み方
acknowledge uncertainty、recognize serious harm、admit specific error と役割を分け、最後にupdate可能なjudgmentへ戻しています。

Reading
Treatment decisions often involve more than identifying which medical option is technically available. A clinician may select several reasonable treatments based on diagnosis, age, other conditions, and previous treatment. However, selecting appropriate options is not the same as choosing which one a particular patient should receive.


Different options can involve different trade-offs. One treatment may offer a higher chance of improvement but also a greater risk of side effects. Another may be less intensive but require a longer period of recovery. A patient may value avoiding hospitalization, maintaining daily activities, or reducing uncertainty differently from another person with the same diagnosis.


For this reason, information needs to be understandable. Listing probabilities and possible complications is not enough if the patient cannot connect them to the decision. Clinicians may need to explain what a percentage means, compare options in absolute rather than vague terms, and check whether the patient has understood the main differences.


The patient's role also matters. A clinician can recommend an option, but the patient may still choose another reasonable treatment or opt for no immediate intervention when observation is medically acceptable. This does not mean that every preference is medically appropriate. It means that more than one acceptable option can exist and that personal priorities may help determine which one fits best.


Pressure can weaken the quality of the decision. A patient who feels unable to disagree with a clinician may sign a form without making a genuinely voluntary choice. Time, opportunity to ask questions, and permission to reconsider can therefore matter. Decision aids can help when several options are reasonable. A simple table may compare expected benefit, common side effects, recovery time, and how each option affects daily life. The purpose is not to choose for the patient but to make the trade-offs easier to see. Preferences can also change after a person reflects on what matters most or talks with family, so a decision may need to be revisited rather than treated as fixed after one conversation.


Shared decision-making separates professional selection from personal choice. Clinicians help identify which options are medically reasonable and explain their expected benefits and risks. Patients then choose among those options according to their own priorities, provided they understand the relevant information and are free to decide.

### この問題で考えること
「医学的に可能な選択肢」と「本人にとって最も合う選択肢」は同じとは限りません。**clinical eligibilityとpatient preferenceを別の判断段階として扱う**ことが核心です。

### 考え方
clinician selects reasonable options
→ benefit / risk / daily-life effect を説明
→ patient understands trade-offs
→ personal prioritiesを使ってchoose / opt for
→ voluntary decision

### 弱い考え方との比較
× The doctor selects the best treatment.
○ The clinician may identify several medically reasonable options, while the patient chooses among them according to informed priorities.

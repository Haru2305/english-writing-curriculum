Reading
Modern food supply chains are often designed to move goods quickly while keeping storage costs low. Supermarkets do not want large amounts of perishable food sitting unused, and companies can reduce waste by matching deliveries closely to expected demand. In normal conditions, this kind of efficiency can lower costs for both businesses and consumers.


The same system can become vulnerable when a major disruption occurs. If a store depends on one distribution center or a producer depends on one supplier, there may be few alternatives when transport stops or a facility closes. Low inventory can make the shortage appear quickly because there is little extra stock to absorb the delay.


This creates a trade-off between efficiency and resilience. Keeping larger inventories or using several suppliers can increase ordinary operating costs. Yet redundancy may mitigate the impact of a disruption by giving companies more time to adjust. A second supplier that seems unnecessary in normal weeks can become valuable when the main supplier cannot deliver.


Consumer behavior can also amplify shortages. If people expect an essential product to disappear, they may buy more than usual. For one household, this can seem rational. When many households act at the same time, however, demand can rise suddenly and empty shelves can encourage even more buying. Stores may limit purchases temporarily to curb panic buying and ease pressure on supply.


Not every product requires the same strategy. Large inventories are expensive and can increase waste when food spoils. For products that are easy to replace, maintaining many suppliers may provide little benefit. For essential goods with few substitutes, the cost of additional resilience may be easier to justify.


Information can help as well. Better data about inventory, transport delays, and expected demand can allow firms to redirect goods before shortages become severe. Clear communication may also alleviate unnecessary concern among consumers if people know when new supplies are expected. However, information cannot remove every physical bottleneck. A company may know exactly where a shortage is developing and still be unable to move enough goods if roads are closed or alternative suppliers lack capacity. For this reason, data work best when they are combined with physical options such as reserve stock, multiple transport routes, or contracts with more than one supplier.


The most resilient system is therefore not simply the one with the most stock. It is one that balances cost, waste, alternative suppliers, transport options, and the importance of the product. That balance can differ across products and regions. The most efficient design in normal times may not be the design that performs best during a disruption.

2. A mitigate

Why these words?
本文の mitigate the impact は「disruptionを防ぐ」ではありません。
ease pressure はsystemへの圧力を和らげる表現です。
このように「何が減るのか」で動詞を選びます。

解説補強｜効率性とresilienceを同じ尺度で測らない

### 考え方
low inventory / single supplier は平時コストを下げても、disruption時にbufferが小さくなります。つまり、**normal-time efficiencyを上げる仕組みが、shock absorptionを弱めることがある**のがMechanismです。

### 弱い考え方との比較
× Extra inventory is inefficient, so it should always be minimized.
○ Some reserve capacity may raise normal costs while buying time to respond during disruption.

### 模範解答の読み方
reduce storage costs がBenefit、shortages appear quickly がMechanism、extra suppliers / information がSupport・mitigationです。mitigate は「riskを消す」のではなく、**失敗時のimpactを小さくする**語として論理と一致しています。

### 次の問題に持っていくもの
効率化を評価するときは、**何を削った結果、どのbuffer・alternative・response timeが減るか**を確認します。

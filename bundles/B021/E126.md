Source A

[1] A public sports center wants to make beginner programs accessible without creating rules that are impossible to administer. The current policy gives reduced fees to residents below a defined income threshold. This rule is transparent and easy to apply.



[2] Staff have identified one limitation. Some residents face temporary financial difficulty that is not reflected in the previous year’s income record—for example, after sudden job loss or a major change in household circumstances. A strict historical-income rule may therefore exclude people whose current ability to pay has changed sharply.



[3] This does not mean that anyone who reports financial difficulty should automatically receive a reduction. The center needs evidence that can be checked consistently. A temporary exception might require recent proof of income loss and might apply for a limited period before eligibility is reviewed again.



[4] Accessibility also depends on whether people know the exception exists. A carefully designed exception has little effect if information about it is available only after a resident is rejected under the ordinary rule. The center should therefore explain both the standard rule and the limited exception at the same point of application.



[5] The broader principle is that transparent rules can remain useful even when they do not represent every case perfectly. Exceptions are strongest when they are narrow, evidence-based, time-limited where appropriate, and visible to the people who may need them.



Source B

Program manager:

“If the exception is too broad, staff will struggle to apply it consistently and residents may not understand why similar cases receive different decisions.”



Community adviser:

“A rule is not genuinely accessible if people who qualify for an exception learn about it only after they give up applying.”

Reading answers

Q1｜解答例

The rule can fail when a resident’s current financial situation has changed sharply but the historical income record has not yet captured that change.



Q2｜解答例

The manager emphasizes inconsistent or overly broad application, while the community adviser emphasizes the risk that eligible residents will never learn that the exception exists.



Q3

Group B: residents with documented recent income change who did not know the exception existed used it far less (14%) than Group A (68%), who had the same documented change and knew about it.



Q4｜解答例

Because a narrow, evidence-based exception can improve the fit between the rule and its purpose without making decisions arbitrary.

Model summary

The sports center’s income rule is transparent, but historical records can miss residents whose finances have changed suddenly. A limited exception can improve access if it uses checkable evidence and is applied consistently. The exception should also be visible when residents apply, because eligibility has little value if people do not know it exists. Fairness therefore depends on both narrow qualification rules and practical access to them.



Model answer

The center should keep the standard income rule but add a temporary exception for residents who can show recent major income loss. Source A supports narrow, evidence-based exceptions, while the program manager warns that broad discretion would create inconsistent decisions. The table shows that residents with documented income change used the exception far less often when they were unaware of it (14% versus 68%), so the center should also display the exception beside the normal eligibility rule. Applications should be reviewed after a limited period so that the exception responds to current hardship without becoming an undefined permanent category. This keeps the exception narrow and administratively reviewable.

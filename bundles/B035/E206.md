Passage

People often assume that more information leads to better decisions. In many situations this is sensible. A doctor needs relevant clinical data, a traveler benefits from accurate schedules, and a voter may want to compare competing proposals. But the relationship between information and judgment is not simply “more is better.”

One reason is that attention is limited. When dozens of indicators are displayed at once, users may focus on the most vivid number rather than the most relevant one. A dashboard can therefore create an appearance of comprehensiveness while making it harder to see which measures actually answer the question at hand.

Another difficulty is that information arrives at different levels of reliability. A carefully measured figure may appear beside an early estimate, a personal report, or a prediction generated by a model. If these are presented in the same visual format, readers may unconsciously treat them as equally certain.

The problem is not solved merely by removing information. Hiding uncertainty can be just as misleading as displaying too much detail. What matters is structure: users need cues that show which information is central, how certain it is, and what decision it can reasonably support.

Consider a hospital waiting-time display. Patients may want to know how long they are likely to wait, but a single number can conceal variation. A stated wait of “30 minutes” may be a recent average, a current estimate, or a guaranteed maximum. Each meaning supports a different expectation. Adding several unlabeled numbers would not necessarily help. A better display might show one main estimate, a range, and a short note explaining why the estimate may change.

This example illustrates a broader principle. Good communication is not the transfer of the maximum possible quantity of information. It is the design of a path through information. The communicator decides what the audience should notice first, what uncertainty must remain visible, and what details can be postponed until they become relevant.

Such design inevitably involves judgment. There is no universal amount of information that is optimal for every audience or task. Experts may need access to raw data, while members of the public may first need a clear summary. The goal is not to protect one group from complexity but to organize complexity so that users can enter it at an appropriate level.

The paradox is that simplification can support deeper understanding when it is reversible. A clear summary is useful if readers can inspect the underlying detail when needed. Simplification becomes dangerous when it removes the possibility of checking how the summary was produced.

Questions

1. What is meant by “an appearance of comprehensiveness” in paragraph 2?

2. In Japanese, explain in 50–70 characters why displaying several numbers in the same format can mislead readers.

3. Choose the best paraphrase of “the design of a path through information.”
   A. hiding difficult facts from users
   B. arranging information so users encounter relevant levels in a useful order
   C. increasing the amount of available data
   D. replacing expert judgment with automated summaries

4. In Japanese, explain why a “30-minute wait” can support different expectations.

5. What does the author mean by saying simplification should be “reversible”?

6. Decide T or F.
   a. The author believes experts and the public should always receive the same presentation.
   b. Removing all uncertainty is presented as a desirable communication goal.
   c. A summary can be useful if users can inspect the detail behind it.
   d. The passage argues that information quantity alone determines communication quality.

7. Write the main claim in 25–35 English words.

解答・解説・自己修正

1｜解答例
A display may look complete because it contains many indicators even though the abundance of data makes the important relationship harder to identify.

2｜解答例
測定値・推定値・個人報告など確実性の異なる情報が同じ見た目だと、読者が同程度に信頼できると誤認しやすいから。

3｜解答
B

4｜解答例
「30分」が直近平均・現在予測・最大保証のどれかで意味が異なり、待ち時間に対する期待や判断も変わるから。

5｜解答例
A simplified summary should allow users to move back to the underlying evidence or detail when they need to check how the summary was formed.

6｜解答
a F / b F / c T / d F

7｜解答例
Effective communication depends not on maximizing information but on structuring reliable, uncertain, summary, and detailed information so users can reach the level appropriate to their decision.

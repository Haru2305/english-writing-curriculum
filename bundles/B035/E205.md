Passage

Cities increasingly use digital maps to decide where to plant trees, build shaded walkways, or place drinking fountains during hot weather. At first glance, the task seems straightforward: identify the hottest places and direct resources there. Yet the apparent precision of a heat map can hide several decisions that strongly influence what the map means.

The first decision concerns measurement. Some maps use air temperature measured by fixed sensors. Others use surface temperature estimated from satellite images. These two quantities are related but not identical. A paved parking lot may have a very hot surface in the afternoon while the air a meter above it is somewhat cooler. A shaded street may show the opposite pattern at another time of day. If a map combines these measurements without explaining the difference, users may believe they are comparing the same thing.

The second decision concerns time. A single image taken at 2 p.m. can reveal where surfaces are hottest at that moment, but it cannot show whether the same location remains uncomfortable in the evening or becomes dangerous during the morning commute. A neighborhood that appears moderate in a daytime average may still experience short periods of extreme heat that matter greatly to elderly residents or outdoor workers.

A third issue is who is represented. Suppose planners rank locations by temperature alone. They may prioritize a hot industrial area where few people walk and overlook a slightly cooler residential street used daily by hundreds of residents. If the purpose is to reduce human exposure rather than simply lower measured temperature, then pedestrian use, age distribution, access to indoor cooling, and travel patterns may all be relevant.

This does not mean that planners should abandon quantitative maps. On the contrary, maps are valuable because they make patterns visible and allow limited budgets to be compared across locations. The problem begins when a map is treated as if it were a neutral photograph of need rather than a model built for a particular question.

One useful response is to construct several layers instead of one final ranking. A temperature layer can show physical heat, a mobility layer can estimate where people spend time outdoors, and a vulnerability layer can indicate where residents have fewer alternatives to heat exposure. Planners can then examine where these layers overlap. Such an approach is more complicated than publishing a single score, but it makes the reasoning behind decisions easier to inspect.

The broader lesson extends beyond urban heat. Whenever a complex social problem is converted into a map, score, or ranking, some features are emphasized and others are suppressed. The relevant question is not whether simplification can be avoided. It cannot. The question is whether the chosen simplification matches the decision the tool is supposed to support.

Questions

1. In Japanese, explain in 45–65 characters why surface temperature and air temperature should not automatically be treated as the same measure.

2. What does “the same thing” in paragraph 2 refer to?

3. Why can a location with a lower measured temperature deserve higher priority than a hotter location?

4. In Japanese, explain the function of paragraph 5 in the overall argument.

5. Decide T or F according to the passage.
   a. A single afternoon heat image can identify every period of dangerous exposure.
   b. The author argues that quantitative maps are useless for urban planning.
   c. A layered map can make the assumptions behind priority decisions more visible.
   d. Simplification is unavoidable when complex problems are represented.

6. State the main point of the passage in one English sentence of 20–30 words.

Self-check

□ T/Fは一文ずつ本文根拠を確認した
□ 主旨を「地図は危険」ではなく「目的との適合」で取った

解答・解説・自己修正

1｜解答例
地表面の熱さと人が感じる高さの気温は関連しても同一ではなく、場所や時刻によって差が生じるから。

2｜解答
It refers to surface temperature and air temperature as if they were identical measurements.

3｜解答例
Because the decision may concern human exposure. A slightly cooler place used by many vulnerable people can create greater practical need than a hotter place with little pedestrian activity.

4｜解答例
数値地図そのものを否定するのではなく、地図は特定の問いに合わせて作られたモデルであり、目的と指標の対応を点検すべきだと論点を整理している。

5｜解答
a F / b F / c T / d T

6｜解答例
Maps are useful for planning only when their measurements and simplifications are matched to the specific decision they are meant to support.

自己修正
筑波型の日本語説明では「根拠→意味」を短くつなぐ。T/Fは部分一致で決めない。

Part A｜Reading

A public library is testing a system that predicts how crowded different floors will be during the day. The model uses past entry counts, room reservations, the academic calendar, and weather forecasts.

The predictions are often useful. Students can choose a quieter time to visit, and staff can move support services to periods of higher demand. Yet a prediction can also change behavior. If many students avoid a floor because the system predicts crowding, the floor may end up less crowded than expected.

This creates a feedback problem. The model was trained on behavior that occurred before users saw the prediction. Once the prediction becomes public, it may influence the very behavior it is trying to forecast.

The library therefore avoids describing the number as a guaranteed forecast. It presents a range and states that accuracy may fall during unusual events. Staff also compare predicted and actual crowding after each week and update the model.

The broader point is that a predictive tool becomes part of the system once people act on it. Evaluating the tool therefore requires asking not only whether its earlier predictions were accurate, but also how publication of those predictions changes later behavior.

Questions

1. In Japanese, explain in 45–65 characters what the “feedback problem” is.

2. Why does the library present a range rather than a guaranteed number?

3. T/F

   b. Publishing a prediction can affect later crowding.
   c. The author says past accuracy is sufficient to judge the tool.
   d. The library checks predictions against actual results.

Model writing (91 words)
The library should offer daily crowding forecasts, but students should be able to choose whether to receive them. The forecasts could help users avoid busy periods and may also help staff manage demand. However, the passage shows that predictions can change behavior, so a widely distributed forecast may make the original model less accurate. The message should therefore present a range rather than a guaranteed number and explain that unusual events can reduce accuracy. The library should also compare predictions with actual crowding and update the model regularly.

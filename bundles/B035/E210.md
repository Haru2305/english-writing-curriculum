Part A｜Reading

Public forecasts can change the systems they describe. A library, for example, may publish a prediction that one floor will be crowded in the afternoon. Students who see the prediction may choose another floor, making the original prediction less accurate.

This does not make forecasting useless. The forecast may still achieve a practical goal by spreading demand. However, the institution must distinguish between a prediction that accurately describes what would have happened without publication and a prediction that successfully changes behavior after publication.

A similar problem appears in traffic applications. If an app recommends the same alternative road to thousands of drivers, that road may become congested. The recommendation has become part of the traffic system rather than a neutral description of it.

[ A ]

For this reason, evaluation should include both prediction accuracy and behavioral response. A forecast can be valuable even when publication changes the final outcome, but users should understand that the number is conditional rather than guaranteed.

Part C｜Writing

A university has decided to provide students with daily forecasts of library crowding. Publishing a forecast may itself change where students go, so the displayed prediction can affect the outcome it describes.

What rule should the university adopt for how these forecasts are shown or used so that they are helpful without creating a new problem?

Write about 100 English words. Propose one clear rule, explain the benefit and one risk it addresses, and include one concrete example or safeguard.

The forecast should be treated as conditional because publishing it can alter the behavior that the forecast is meant to predict.

Model writing (103 words)
The university should show crowding forecasts as ranges, such as low, medium, or high, rather than as guaranteed numbers. This would help students choose quieter spaces while reminding them that the forecast itself may change behavior. If everyone sees that one floor will be crowded, many students may move elsewhere and create new congestion. The system should therefore update forecasts after major shifts and compare predictions with actual use. Presenting the information as conditional would preserve its practical value without suggesting false precision. Students could still use the forecast, but they would understand that their responses become part of the system being predicted.

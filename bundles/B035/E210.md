Part A｜Reading

Public forecasts can change the systems they describe. A library, for example, may publish a prediction that one floor will be crowded in the afternoon. Students who see the prediction may choose another floor, making the original prediction less accurate.

This does not make forecasting useless. The forecast may still achieve a practical goal by spreading demand. However, the institution must distinguish between a prediction that accurately describes what would have happened without publication and a prediction that successfully changes behavior after publication.

A similar problem appears in traffic applications. If an app recommends the same alternative road to thousands of drivers, that road may become congested. The recommendation has become part of the traffic system rather than a neutral description of it.

[ A ]

For this reason, evaluation should include both prediction accuracy and behavioral response. A forecast can be valuable even when publication changes the final outcome, but users should understand that the number is conditional rather than guaranteed.

The forecast should not be treated as if behavior could not change.

Model writing (98 words)
I think the university should provide crowding forecasts, but students should be able to turn the messages off. The information could help students choose quieter times and may spread demand across the day. However, the passage shows that forecasts can change the behavior they predict. For example, if everyone receives the same warning about one floor, too many students may move to another floor. The university should therefore show a range rather than a guaranteed number and compare predictions with actual crowding. If the forecasts repeatedly create new congestion, the system should be adjusted.

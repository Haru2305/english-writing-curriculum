Passage

Museums once tended to present collections as stable sets of objects accompanied by authoritative labels. Digital archives have changed that expectation. A visitor can now search thousands of images, compare several descriptions, and sometimes view earlier versions of a catalog entry.

This increased access has an unexpected consequence: disagreement becomes more visible. An object may have been dated differently by two researchers, or a place of origin may be uncertain. In a printed gallery label, such disagreement was often compressed into one short statement. A digital record has more space to preserve competing interpretations.

More space, however, does not automatically produce better understanding. A page that lists five possible dates without explaining why they differ may simply confuse readers. The archive must decide how to distinguish an established fact, a probable interpretation, and an unresolved possibility.

One method is to separate layers of description. The first layer gives a concise current interpretation. A second layer shows the evidence behind it, such as material analysis or historical documents. A third records previous interpretations and explains why they changed.

This layered structure does not eliminate disagreement. Instead, it makes disagreement inspectable. A visitor can see both what the museum currently believes and how strong the basis for that belief is.

The approach also changes the meaning of correction. Updating a catalog entry no longer has to imply that earlier researchers were careless. New evidence may become available, or a new method may reveal information that could not previously be measured.

For this reason, a well-designed digital archive can present knowledge as revisable without presenting it as arbitrary. The challenge is to show that uncertainty has structure: some claims are strongly supported, some are provisional, and some remain genuinely open.

Questions

1. In Japanese, explain in 45–65 characters why greater digital space does not automatically improve understanding.

2. What does “This layered structure” refer to?

3. In Japanese, explain the difference between “revisable” and “arbitrary” as used in the final paragraph.

4. T/F
   a. Digital archives necessarily remove disagreement among researchers.
   b. Earlier catalog entries may change because new methods become available.
   c. The author recommends presenting all interpretations without indicating their evidential strength.
   d. Layered description can show both a current interpretation and its basis.

English Output
In 70–90 English words, explain why recording earlier interpretations can be useful rather than embarrassing for a museum.

English Output｜Model
Recording earlier interpretations shows how knowledge developed rather than simply displaying past mistakes. A museum can explain what evidence supported an older view and what new evidence later changed it. This helps visitors understand that scholarly conclusions are revisable but not arbitrary. It also makes the strength of current claims easier to evaluate, because readers can see the reasoning and evidence behind a correction instead of receiving only the newest label.

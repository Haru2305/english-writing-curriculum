Reading

[1] Efficient supply systems often try to minimize unused capacity. Empty warehouse space, duplicate suppliers, and spare transport routes can appear wasteful because they cost money even when nothing goes wrong. In stable conditions, reducing this unused capacity may lower prices and simplify coordination.



[2] The same features, however, can play a different role when disruption occurs. A second supplier that seemed unnecessary under normal conditions may become valuable if the main supplier suddenly cannot deliver. Extra storage can make it possible to continue production while a transport problem is resolved. Economists and planners sometimes describe this kind of unused capacity as redundancy.



[3] Redundancy is therefore not simply “waste.” It is capacity that may appear unnecessary until an unexpected event makes it useful. The difficulty is deciding how much redundancy is worth paying for. Too little can make a system fragile; too much can make ordinary operation unnecessarily expensive.



[4] A useful comparison is the spare battery carried by a field researcher. Most of the time, the second battery does nothing. Judged only by average daily use, it looks inefficient. Yet its value becomes clear on the day when recharging is impossible. The example does not imply that every trip requires ten spare batteries. It shows why the value of backup capacity depends on both the probability and the cost of failure.



[5] This matters in international supply chains because firms often depend on resources that cross several borders before reaching a factory or consumer. A delay in one location can affect production elsewhere. Diversifying suppliers, maintaining some inventory, or designing products that can use alternative components can reduce dependence on one route. Each measure creates costs, but those costs may be justified when interruption would be especially damaging.



[6] The broader point is that efficiency should be defined relative to the risks a system must face. A system that minimizes cost under perfect conditions may perform poorly under disruption. Conversely, a system designed for every imaginable disaster may be too expensive to operate. The goal is not maximum redundancy but an appropriate balance between ordinary efficiency and resilience.

Reading answers

Q1｜B

Paragraph 2〜3が、平時には不要に見えるが disruption 時に役立つ backup capacity と定義している。



Q2｜解答例

Efficient systems need an appropriate balance between low normal operating cost and enough backup capacity to remain functional when disruptions occur.



Q3｜解答例

It turns the abstract idea of redundancy into a concrete case, showing why unused capacity can still have value while also showing that more backup is not always better.



Q4｜解答例

The author rejects it as an extreme because preparing for every imaginable failure could make normal operation too expensive.

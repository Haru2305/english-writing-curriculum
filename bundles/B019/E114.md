Reading

[1] Food supply discussions often focus on average cost, but a system that is inexpensive in ordinary weeks may still be vulnerable to short disruptions. One way firms respond is by maintaining substitution options: alternative ingredients, packaging, suppliers, or routes that can be used when the usual choice becomes unavailable.



[2] Such options are not free. Alternative suppliers may charge more, and products designed to accept several components may be more complex to produce. The value of substitution therefore depends on the cost of interruption as well as the cost of maintaining flexibility.



[3] A simple example is a school cafeteria that normally buys one type of vegetable from a single distributor. If the menu can use two or three similar vegetables without changing its nutritional purpose, a temporary shortage of one item need not cancel the meal. This flexibility does not eliminate the shortage; it changes the consequence of the shortage.



[4] That distinction is important. A resilient system is not one in which disruption never occurs. It is one in which the system can continue an important function despite some disruption. For this reason, measuring only how often a supply problem occurs can miss whether the organization has reduced the effect of that problem.



[5] Firms may therefore describe substitution capacity as a form of resilience. The claim should still be limited: having alternatives may reduce dependence on one input, but it does not guarantee continuous supply under every type of disruption. Severe shortages can affect several alternatives at the same time.



Notice

Pilot purchasing rule:

For eight weeks, one cafeteria will be allowed to substitute from an approved list of three vegetables when the standard item is unavailable. Prices, meal schedule, and nutritional requirements will remain unchanged.

Reading answers

Q1｜解答例

Alternative inputs, suppliers, routes, or other choices that can replace the usual one when it becomes unavailable.



Q2

It refers to the distinction between preventing a disruption from occurring and reducing its consequences by continuing an important function despite disruption.



Q3｜解答例

It turns the abstract idea of substitution capacity into a concrete case and shows how flexibility can change the consequence of a shortage without eliminating the shortage itself.



Q4｜解答例

Because alternatives can reduce dependence but cannot guarantee supply under every disruption; several alternatives may fail at the same time.

Model answer

Alternative supply options can make a system more resilient because they allow an organization to continue an important function when the usual input is unavailable. However, maintaining several options can increase cost or complexity, so more alternatives are not always more efficient. The cafeteria pilot can test whether a limited substitution list reduces cancelled or changed meals without increasing cost too much. If it succeeds, the result would suggest that substitution improves resilience under some shortages, not that every supply problem can be completely avoided.

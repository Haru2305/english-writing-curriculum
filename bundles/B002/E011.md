Reading
Disaster preparedness is difficult to evaluate because success is often invisible. When a flood barrier works, an evacuation system functions, or backup power prevents a hospital from closing, the main result may be damage that never occurs. This makes preventive investment politically and psychologically different from repairing visible destruction after a disaster.


After a major event, the benefits of spending are easy to see. Roads need to be rebuilt, damaged equipment needs to be replaced, and affected families may need immediate support. Before an event, however, decision makers must invest in systems whose value depends on uncertain future risks. Some people therefore argue against large preparedness budgets when no recent disaster has occurred.


That reasoning can be misleading. A quiet year does not show that preparation was unnecessary. It may simply mean that the dangerous event did not occur. Even when a disaster does happen, good preparation can reduce the damage. Early warnings can lead to faster evacuation, stronger buildings can reduce injuries, and backup systems can allow essential services to continue.


The challenge is that avoided losses cannot be observed directly. If a hospital invests in backup power and never loses electricity, it is hard to know how much harm was prevented. Analysts often compare likely outcomes under different scenarios. They may estimate what would happen with and without a measure rather than wait for repeated disasters to provide evidence.


Maintenance is another problem. Governments may provide equipment after a crisis but fail to invest resources in inspection, training, or replacement. A warning system that is not tested can appear to exist while offering little real protection. Preparedness therefore includes not only purchasing equipment but also keeping people and systems ready to use it.


This does not mean every preventive project is worthwhile. Resources are limited, and some risks are too small to justify very expensive measures. The better argument for preparedness is not that all possible dangers must be eliminated. It is that high-consequence risks should be compared with the cost and effectiveness of realistic safeguards.


For this reason, disaster policy often requires decisions under uncertainty. A community may never be able to prove exactly how much damage a measure will prevent. It can still ask whether the measure is likely to reduce serious harm, whether the cost is reasonable, and whether the system will be maintained. Investment before a crisis should be judged partly by losses avoided, not only by visible benefits produced.

Transfer Writing
モデル答案
Communities should invest in preparedness before a disaster occurs. Stronger buildings and warning systems can contribute to lower losses even when their exact effect is difficult to observe. I would argue for maintaining these systems when the possible harm is large and the cost is reasonable.


別解
A quiet period does not prove that preparedness is unnecessary. Investment in backup systems may lead to faster recovery when a crisis eventually occurs. The value of prevention should therefore include losses that are avoided.

解説補強｜見えない成果をどう評価するか

### この問題で考えること
「災害が起きなかった＝対策は不要だった」とは限りません。予防では、**観察された結果だけでなく、対策がなかった場合のcounterfactualを考える**必要があります。

### 考え方
preparedness → warning / evacuation / backup capacity
→ disaster発生時のharmを減らし得る
ただし disaster自体が起きなければ avoided loss は直接見えない
→ with / without measure のscenario比較が必要

### 弱い考え方との比較
× Nothing happened, so the investment was wasted.
○ A quiet year provides little evidence about whether preparedness would reduce harm in a severe event.

### 模範解答の読み方
invest in は手段、contribute to / lead to は因果の強さ、argue for は判断を表します。最後に cost と expected harm reduction を比較して、無条件の「予防は善」にはしていません。

### 次の問題に持っていくもの
予防策では **baseline risk / counterfactual / avoided harm / maintenance / cost** を確認します。

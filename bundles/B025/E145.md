Reading
[1] Some consumer products are difficult to repair even when the broken part itself is inexpensive. A battery may be glued into place, a case may require a special tool to open, or replacement parts may be sold only to authorized repair centers. To make these differences visible, a fictional city introduced a “repairability score” for selected electronic products sold through public procurement programs.
[2] The score does not measure whether a product will break. Instead, it describes how practical repair is after a fault occurs. Evaluators consider whether the product can be opened without destructive steps, whether common components can be replaced separately, whether repair instructions are available, and whether replacement parts can be obtained for several years.
[3] A high score can therefore mean several different things. One device may be easy to open but have expensive parts. Another may require more time to disassemble but use standardized components available from many suppliers. The score combines several features, so two products with the same total may have different repair profiles.
[4] This creates a communication problem. If buyers treat the score as a simple measure of product quality, they may overinterpret it. A low-scoring product could still be reliable and rarely need repair. A high-scoring product could be easy to fix but fail more often. The score is useful only if users understand the specific question it answers.
[5] Supporters argue that such a score can change incentives. If organizations consider repairability when purchasing equipment, manufacturers may have a reason to make parts easier to replace or documentation easier to obtain. However, the effect depends on whether buyers actually use the score in decisions rather than merely displaying it.
[6] Critics also note that a single number can hide trade-offs. A sealed case may make repair harder while improving resistance to water or dust. Longer parts availability may increase inventory costs. These trade-offs do not make the score meaningless, but they show why the number should be read together with its component information.
[7] The broader lesson is that an index can clarify a hidden feature of a product only when its scope is clear. Repairability is not the same as reliability, price, environmental impact, or overall quality. The score is most useful as one piece of evidence within a larger decision.

Model output
A repairability score describes how practical it is to fix a product after something fails. It may consider how easily the product opens, whether parts can be replaced separately, and whether instructions and components remain available. However, the score does not measure reliability or overall quality. Two products with the same score may also have different repair strengths and trade-offs.
## 解説の考え方

### この問題で考えること
repairability score を「製品の良さを表す点数」と読むのではなく、**何を測る指標で、何を測らない指標か**を先に決める。

### 考え方
未知の index は **definition → components → nearby concepts との区別 → trade-off → decision での使い方** の順に組み立てる。ここでは score は「故障後に修理しやすいか」を表し、reliability や overall quality ではない。同じ総合点でも構成要素が異なるため、total score だけから具体的な強みまで逆算できない。

### 弱い考え方との比較
× 「高得点だから壊れにくく、良い製品」  
○ 「高得点は修理しやすさを示すが、故障率・価格・性能は別に確認する」  
改善点は、**indicator の scope を結論の scope と一致させる**こと。

### 模範解答の読み方
Model output は definition → components → contrast with nearby concepts → limitation の順。3文目で repairability を reliability / overall quality と切り分け、4文目で「同じ総合点でも中身は異なりうる」という score の限界を示している。3文目と4文目の両方が、score の意味を product quality 全体へ広げすぎないための働きをしている。

### 次の問題に持っていくもの
新しい score / index が出たら、**何を足し合わせた数値か、隣接概念と何が違うか、一つの数値で隠れるtrade-offは何か**を確認する。

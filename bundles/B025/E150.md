Passage
A fictional electricity program uses a “flexibility credit.” Households receive a small credit when they reduce selected electricity use during a short period of unusually high demand. The program does not require them to use less electricity overall; some activities can simply be moved to an earlier or later time.
The idea is based on a distinction between total consumption and peak demand. Electricity systems must have enough capacity to meet the highest simultaneous demand, even if that level lasts only briefly. [A] Reducing or shifting some flexible use during those periods can therefore lower pressure on the system without asking households to stop essential activities.
Not every household has the same ability to shift use. A person working from home may need cooling and computer power at a fixed time. Another household may be able to delay a dishwasher or electric-vehicle charging with little inconvenience. For this reason, the program treats participation as optional rather than assuming that every user has equal flexibility.
The size of a credit also matters. If it is too small, few people may change behavior. If it is very large, the program may pay more than the avoided peak cost is worth. The purpose is therefore not to maximize participation at any price but to obtain useful demand reduction at a reasonable cost.
Candidate Sentence
Peak demand is therefore partly a timing problem, not simply a problem of how much electricity is used in a day.
FAQ
Q: Must households reduce total daily electricity use?
A: No. Shifting flexible use outside the target period can count.
Q: Are essential medical or safety-related uses restricted?
A: No. Participation is voluntary, and the program does not require interruption of essential use.
Q: Does a household receive the same credit every day?
A: No. Credits are offered only during selected high-demand periods.

解答・解説・自己修正
Q1｜解答例
A flexibility credit is a small reward offered during selected high-demand periods when a household voluntarily reduces or shifts flexible electricity use away from that period.
Q2｜解答
A. The preceding sentences introduce total consumption versus peak demand, and the Candidate Sentence names the resulting timing distinction. The next sentence then explains the operational response: shifting flexible use.
Q3｜解答例
A higher participation rate does not necessarily mean a larger reduction per participant. Group B has the highest participation but the lowest average reduction, while Group C shows the opposite pattern.
Q4｜解答例
The program may shift the timing of use rather than reduce total daily consumption, and essential uses are not required to stop.
Q5｜解答例
System pressure can sometimes be reduced by changing when flexible demand occurs rather than by maximizing total energy reduction.
Model summary
A flexibility credit rewards households for voluntarily reducing or shifting nonessential electricity use during selected peak periods. The program targets timing rather than total daily consumption and does not require interruption of essential uses. Participation and reduction also vary across groups: more participants do not necessarily produce a larger reduction per household. The approach may lower peak pressure, but households differ in how easily they can shift electricity use.
Model writing
The program should continue only if it produces meaningful peak-demand reduction at a reasonable cost. The town should measure total reduction during target periods, not participation alone, because the table shows that groups with more participants may reduce less per household. It should also track which households can participate without disrupting essential activities so that the program does not reward only those with highly flexible schedules. However, the current table does not show program costs or long-term behavior, so a longer evaluation is needed before judging overall value. That evidence would support a fairer decision.

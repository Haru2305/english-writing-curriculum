Passage A
Coastal towns facing repeated erosion sometimes build hard barriers such as sea walls. Supporters argue that a barrier can protect roads, buildings, and other fixed infrastructure where moving those assets is unrealistic. A wall also creates a visible line of defense, which can make planning easier when a town must decide exactly which area it intends to protect.
The limitation is that a fixed barrier does not remove wave energy. In some settings, water reflected from a hard surface can change erosion nearby, and a beach in front of the structure may continue to narrow. Maintenance also matters because a barrier designed for past conditions may later need strengthening. The strongest case for a wall is therefore usually site-specific: valuable fixed assets, limited space, and a clear willingness to maintain the structure over time.
Passage B
Other coastal planners prefer approaches that allow the shoreline to move while reducing damage. Adding sand, restoring dunes, or moving vulnerable facilities inland can preserve a wider transition zone between land and water. These approaches may support recreation or habitat as well as protection, and they avoid committing every location to a permanent hard edge.
But flexibility also has costs. Added sand can wash away and require repeated replacement, while moving infrastructure may be politically or financially difficult. A flexible strategy is not simply “doing nothing.” It depends on monitoring change and accepting that the protected line may shift. Its strongest case is where space exists for adjustment and where preserving beach or habitat functions is part of the objective.
Passage A continued
A hard barrier can also shift decisions into the future. Protecting a road today may postpone a more difficult choice about relocation, but the barrier may also buy time for a town to plan that choice gradually. Whether delay is a benefit or a cost depends on what the town does with the time gained.
Passage B continued
Adaptable approaches face a similar problem in reverse. Leaving room for shoreline movement can preserve options, but only if land-use rules actually prevent new vulnerable development from occupying that space later. Flexibility on a map is not useful if later decisions remove the room needed for adjustment.

Comparison Map｜例

Reading answers
Q1｜B
Aはmaintenance、Bはmonitoring/replacementを必要とし、どちらも一度作れば終わりとは言わない。
Q2｜A
両者の違いをstrategy名ではなく、space / fixed assets / acceptable movementという条件で表している。
Q3｜解答例
Passage A sees hard barriers as strongest where valuable fixed assets must be protected and space is limited. Passage B favors adaptable approaches where the shoreline has room to move and wider beach or habitat functions matter.
Q4｜解答例
Passage A is especially explicit because it says “The strongest case for a wall is therefore usually site-specific,” then lists the relevant conditions.
別解：Passage B も “Its strongest case is where space exists for adjustment …” と条件を明示しているので、その表現を引用して条件付きだと説明できれば可。A は “site-specific” という語そのものを使う点でより明示的。

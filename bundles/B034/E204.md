Passage
[1] Organizations often want a single score that summarizes a complicated system. A transport agency may publish one reliability percentage, a hospital may report one waiting-time target, and a school may combine several outcomes into an overall performance index. Such measures are attractive because they make comparison easy.

[2] The difficulty is that summary scores compress distinctions. Two systems can receive the same overall score while failing in different ways. One may perform consistently at a moderate level, while another alternates between excellent and very poor performance.

[3] A fictional rail network illustrates the problem. Its published “service reliability score” combines on-time arrival, cancellation rate, and recovery after disruption. The overall score improved from 82 to 86 after a timetable change.

Table
Component | Before | After
On-time arrival | 84 | 90
Cancellation control | 88 | 87
Recovery after disruption | 74 | 81

[4] The improvement is real at the level of the index, but the components did not all move in the same direction. Cancellation control declined slightly while the other two components improved.

Candidate Sentence
A higher aggregate score can therefore represent genuine progress without implying that every underlying dimension improved.

[5] The agency’s internal memo adds another complication. The timetable change was introduced during a period with fewer severe storms than the previous year. Managers cannot tell from the before-and-after comparison alone how much of the gain came from the timetable and how much from easier operating conditions.

Internal Memo
“Use the new score for monitoring, but do not describe the timetable change as the sole cause of improvement until performance has been compared across similar weather conditions.”

Dense Sentence
Because an index is useful precisely by reducing several dimensions to a common scale, responsible interpretation requires restoring enough of the hidden component structure to determine whether the apparent improvement matches the decision one intends to make.

[6] The lesson is not that summary measures should be rejected. They are useful for monitoring and communication. But a score should be treated as the beginning of analysis rather than the end when important decisions depend on what changed beneath it.

解答・解説・自己修正

Q1｜解答
After paragraph 4. The sentence summarizes the contrast between the improved overall index and the mixed movement of its components, then paragraph 5 adds a separate causal complication.

Q2｜解答例
Cancellation control declined from 88 to 87. This matters because the higher overall score does not mean every component improved, so decisions should inspect which dimensions changed.

Q3｜解答例
An index is useful because it compresses several dimensions into one common scale. But that compression hides differences among components. For important decisions, users must reopen enough of that structure to see whether the improvement is relevant to the decision.

Q4｜解答例
Because the timetable change occurred in a period with fewer severe storms, so operating conditions changed at the same time and may explain part of the improvement.

Q5｜解答例
Summary scores are useful, but important decisions require examining hidden components and alternative explanations rather than treating an improved index as a complete causal conclusion.

Model summary
The rail agency’s reliability index rose after a timetable change, showing overall improvement but hiding mixed component results: on-time performance and disruption recovery improved while cancellation control declined slightly. The index is useful for monitoring, yet it compresses distinctions that may matter for decisions. In addition, the later period had fewer severe storms, so the score increase cannot be attributed entirely to the timetable. The agency should therefore use the index as a starting point for deeper analysis.

Model writing
The agency should keep the new timetable provisionally while collecting stronger comparative evidence. The overall reliability score increased from 82 to 86, and both on-time arrival and recovery after disruption improved, so the change may be useful. However, cancellation control fell slightly, and the later period also had fewer severe storms. Before making the timetable permanent, the agency should compare performance across periods with similar weather and examine each component separately. If the gains in punctuality and recovery remain without a meaningful increase in cancellations, retaining the timetable would be justified. That would justify adoption more confidently.

Reading
Tourism can provide jobs, tax revenue, and demand for local businesses, yet the same success can create problems when visitors are concentrated in a small area or season. Cities that depend heavily on tourism therefore face a difficult question: how can they preserve economic benefits without allowing congestion to reduce residents' quality of life or damage the experience that attracts visitors in the first place?


Crowding has several causes. The total number of visitors matters, but timing and location can matter just as much. A city may have enough accommodation and transport capacity across an entire year while experiencing severe pressure during a few summer weeks or in a small historic district. Policies that focus only on annual visitor totals can therefore miss the actual source of congestion.


One response is to spread demand. Cities may promote less crowded neighborhoods, encourage travel outside peak season, or adjust opening hours at major attractions. These measures can reduce pressure without necessarily reducing the total number of visitors. However, they work only if alternative places and times are genuinely attractive and accessible.


Another approach is to manage capacity directly. Reservation systems can limit entry to fragile sites, while visitor levies can raise money for cleaning, transport, or infrastructure. Yet such policies can create fairness questions. A high fee may reduce crowding, but it can also make access easier for wealthier visitors than for others.


Residents' needs should also be measured rather than assumed. Some neighborhoods may benefit from tourism income while experiencing little congestion; others may face noise, rising rents, crowded buses, or loss of everyday services. Citywide averages can hide these local differences. The choice of indicator matters as well. Hotel occupancy may show how many visitors stay overnight, but it can miss day-trippers or cruise passengers. Transport data may reveal crowding, while resident surveys may reveal effects that are difficult to observe directly.


Good management therefore requires more than choosing between “more tourism” and “less tourism.” Officials need to identify where and when capacity is exceeded, which groups bear the costs, and whether a policy actually changes the pattern of demand. A measure that sounds strict may achieve little if visitors simply shift to another crowded time or place.


Timing matters in evaluation as well. A policy introduced during one unusual season may appear successful or unsuccessful for reasons unrelated to the policy itself. Cities should compare multiple periods and revise measures when the evidence shows that congestion has moved rather than decreased. They should also define practical success criteria clearly in advance before any formal evaluation begins. If the goal is to reduce crowding on public transport, for example, higher visitor spending alone does not show that the policy worked.

### 再利用する処理手順
時間制約では、全てを速く処理しようとせず、**設問ごとに必要な情報へ時間を割り当てる**ことが中心です。

1. main claim / major contrastを保持
2. question typeから探すtargetを決める
3. target paragraphへ戻る
4. 一問の上限を超えたらmarkして進む
5. 最後にevidenceを確認

### 弱い処理との比較
× 分からない語が出るたび停止する
× 一問で詰まり全文を読み直す
○ 正答に必要な情報だけへ探索範囲を狭める

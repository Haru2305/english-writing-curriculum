Reading
As populations age, many countries are trying to decide how responsibility for long-term care should be shared among families, communities, employers, and public services. Although family members often provide essential support, the assumption that relatives can always absorb additional care work can create problems when households are small or when caregivers also have paid jobs.


A person who helps an older parent may perform many different tasks, including shopping, transport, medication management, and assistance with daily activities. Because these tasks are often spread across the day rather than concentrated into one visible period, the total burden can be difficult for employers or even other family members to recognize. Care that appears to require only a few hours may affect sleep, commuting, and the ability to respond to unexpected events.


Public support can take several forms. Some systems provide professional home-care workers, while others offer cash benefits, respite services, training, or flexible leave for family caregivers. The value of each form depends partly on what problem it is designed to solve. Financial support may help with expenses, but it does not automatically create more time or replace skills that require professional training. Flexible leave can protect employment, yet it may be difficult to use in workplaces where tasks cannot easily be postponed or shared. Respite care can reduce exhaustion, but only if trained workers are available when families need them. A policy can therefore exist formally without producing equal practical access.


Eligibility rules also influence access. A program that requires extensive documentation may direct resources toward people with the greatest needs, yet it can also make support difficult to obtain for families that lack time or information. If different agencies assess health, disability, housing, and income separately, caregivers may spend substantial effort coordinating services that were intended to reduce their burden. Geography creates another layer. A service that is widely available in a large city may be difficult to provide in a rural area where travel times are long and the professional workforce is small. National rules can therefore produce substantially different real-world effects depending on local service capacity.


For this reason, a strong care system does not simply ask whether families or governments should be responsible. It asks which tasks families can reasonably provide, which forms of support reduce avoidable burden, and how services can be organized so that people can actually use them. Care needs can also change over time, so systems need a way to reassess support when a person's condition or family situation changes. Public policy may support family care without assuming that family capacity is unlimited.

### 再利用する処理手順
A26でclause境界を取った後、A01では**hierarchyとlogical relationを保ったまま圧縮**します。

元文をまず、main claim / contrast / condition / reasonへ分けます。その後で選択肢が同じ関係を別語で表しているかを見ます。

### 弱い処理との比較
× 本文と同じ単語が多い選択肢を選ぶ
○ help X but not automatically solve Y のように関係まで圧縮して照合する

### 次の問題に持っていくもの
paraphraseでは **word matchよりrelation match**。長文でもmain clauseを保持したまま比較します。

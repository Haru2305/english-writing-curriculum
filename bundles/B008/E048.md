Text A｜Urban trees and biodiversity
Urban tree planting is often promoted as a way to cool streets and improve local environments. However, projects that focus only on the number of trees planted may overlook whether those trees survive, whether several species are represented, and whether the surrounding habitat supports insects and birds. A city that selects one fast-growing species for every street may achieve a visible result quickly, but a disease that affects that species could later damage much of the urban canopy at once. Survival also varies with soil, water, heat, and maintenance, so the number planted is not the same as the number that will remain healthy ten years later. Some cities therefore track survival rates and habitat value after planting rather than reporting only the initial total. For this reason, planners increasingly consider diversity, long-term maintenance, and connections between parks rather than treating each planting site as an isolated project.


Text B｜Family care and work
Employees who provide care for older relatives may need flexibility even when the total number of caregiving hours appears modest. A medical appointment can occur during work, an unexpected fall can require immediate travel, and fragmented tasks can make schedules difficult to predict. Employers sometimes propose flexible hours or short periods of leave, but policies that exist on paper may be difficult to use if workers believe that requesting them will harm promotion prospects. Effective support therefore depends not only on the formal availability of leave but also on whether employees can use it without unreasonable cost. A policy may look generous if it offers many days of leave, yet workers who lose pay or fear being viewed as unreliable may avoid using it. Managers also need enough staffing flexibility to respond when leave is taken. The practical effect therefore depends on workplace incentives as well as the written rule.


Text C｜Reusable products
Reusable cups and containers can reduce disposable waste when they are used repeatedly. Their environmental value, however, depends on production, cleaning, transport, and the number of times they replace a single-use alternative. A heavy reusable item that is used only twice may perform worse than expected, while a simple product used hundreds of times may provide a clear benefit. Consumers therefore need more than a “reusable” label; they need enough information to understand the conditions under which reuse actually reduces waste. The same cup can perform differently depending on how it is washed, how far it is transported, and how many times it is reused. A system that collects reusable containers but loses many of them after only a few uses may produce less benefit than expected. Clear guidance can therefore help consumers and businesses judge whether repeated use is actually occurring rather than assuming that the product category itself guarantees a good outcome.

Short Writing
モデル
Text C shows that a reusable label is insufficient because environmental benefit depends on repeated use. A product may create little benefit if it rarely replaces disposable items. Consumers therefore need information about how the product should actually be used.


別解
Text B shows that a formal leave policy is insufficient if employees are afraid to use it. Employers may provide flexibility on paper while workplace incentives discourage actual use. Effective support therefore depends on both policy design and practical access.

### このCheckpointで残す一本道

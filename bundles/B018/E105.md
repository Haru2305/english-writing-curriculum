Comparison Guide

Compare:

- goal

- assumed problem

- strongest evidence

- cost / limitation

Text A

Universities should preserve a substantial amount of online teaching even when classrooms are fully available. The strongest reason is not convenience but flexibility of access. Students differ in commuting time, health conditions, work obligations, and the speed at which they can process spoken explanations. Recorded or well-designed online components can give students more control over when and how often they engage with difficult material.



This does not mean that every class should become asynchronous. Discussion, laboratory work, and rapid feedback may depend on real-time interaction. The point is that institutions should not treat physical attendance as the default measure of serious participation when some learning goals can be met through other forms. A mixed system also creates useful redundancy: when travel, illness, or scheduling problems occur, learning need not stop completely.



The main risk is design quality. An online component that merely uploads long videos can increase workload without improving access. Flexibility is valuable only when materials, deadlines, and feedback are organized so that students can actually use it.



Text B

Universities should be cautious about expanding online components simply because they appear flexible. Learning is not only the transfer of information. Students often benefit from routines that require them to prepare, arrive, participate, and respond to other people at a shared time. These structures can be especially important for students who struggle to organize independent study.



The strongest argument for in-person teaching is therefore not tradition but coordination. A scheduled class makes attention, feedback, and peer interaction occur within a common structure. When too much work is moved online, students may gain freedom over timing while losing the external cues that help them begin and sustain difficult tasks.



This does not justify eliminating online materials. Recordings and digital resources can support review and provide backup when students miss class. But the primary schedule should remain visible and shared. Flexibility should supplement, rather than replace, structures that reduce the burden of self-management.

Reading answers

Q1｜解答例

Both want students to have reliable access to learning and to use instructional support effectively.
別解：どちらも学生が効果的に学び、授業に真剣に参加できるようにすることを目指している。



Q2｜解答例

Text A emphasizes barriers such as schedule, travel, health, and processing pace, whereas Text B emphasizes difficulty with self-management and the loss of shared routines.



Q3｜解答例

Text A: online flexibility is valuable only when materials, deadlines, and feedback are organized so that students can actually use it (and discussion or laboratory work may need real-time contact). Text B limits its scope (online materials as backup or supplement) but does not state a success condition for its own recommendation.

WF02｜比較・trade-off

Model answer

A university should decide the balance between online and in-person teaching by asking which barrier is more important for a particular course. Both approaches aim to make learning reliable, but online components reduce schedule and access barriers while shared classes reduce the burden of self-management. Online teaching is stronger when students need flexible review or attendance options, whereas in-person structure is more valuable when interaction and coordinated practice are central. The main trade-off is flexibility versus external structure. Universities should therefore match the format to the course’s learning tasks rather than adopt one rule for every class. This approach avoids treating format as an ideological choice.
